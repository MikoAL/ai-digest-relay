### 1. [FLAMIA: FLAsh MIcroscaling Attention for Fast and Causally Safe Training on Blackwell](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.03344)

HF Daily Papers · paper · efficiency · arXiv:2611.03344
_Fedor Velikonivtsev, Mikhail Koltakov, Denis Kuznedelev_

**In one line:** FLAMIA: MXFP8 and mixed NVFP4/FP8 attention kernels for training on Blackwell, 1.10–1.40x on B200 and 1.06–1.42x on B300

**What:** Low-precision attention for training is hard because block-shared scales can leak information across the causal mask, and forward/backward numerics can drift apart. FLAMIA (Yandex Research) is a set of attention kernels for NVIDIA Blackwell that compute attention in MXFP8 and mixed NVFP4/FP8, cut quantization overhead, choose scales so that no future-token information leaks through shared scales, keep forward and backward computations numerically consistent, and handle variable-length packed documents without repacking. Code is public (github.com/yandex-research/flamia-blackwell-attention).

**Result:** Combined forward+backward attention call is 1.10–1.40x faster on B200 and 1.06–1.42x faster on B300 than the fastest evaluated baseline per workload; the authors say training stability and quality hold when pretraining Llama-style models. An author comment notes that all-FP4 attention adds overhead and slightly worsens loss.

**Critique:** The Hugging Face page does not name the baselines (e.g. whether FlashAttention-4 or cuDNN MXFP8 attention), nor the model sizes, token counts or metrics of the pretraining check, so the end-to-end training gain and the quality claim cannot be judged from it. Speedups are for the attention call only; whole-step speedup will be smaller. Read from the HF page, not the full paper.

### 2. [Integer-Exact Microscaling: Running MXFP4 Language Models on INT8 Matrix Units](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00486)

arXiv · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.00486
_Shinyoung Kang, Junhyung Ahn, Yujun Kim, Jungho Park_

**In one line:** Most MXFP4 weight rows can run as one INT8 GEMM; W4A8 on Apple M5 gives 1.10–1.74x faster prefill than MLX

**What:** On hardware whose fastest path is INT8xINT8 (e.g. Apple M5 GPU Neural Accelerators), MXFP4 kernels apply every 32-element block scale inside the reduction. The authors show that if a weight row's block exponents span at most three, the row equals one INT8 vector times one power-of-two scale, so a linear layer becomes a single INT8 GEMM with one rescale per output; enforcing the condition costs at most 1/16 of a row's largest rounding error, and files stay standard MXFP4. They also define an integer-exact format (16-wide blocks, integer multipliers 1–10, 4.26 bits/weight) that runs as one exact INT8 GEMM.

**Result:** 97.9–99.97% of rows already satisfy the condition across seven models (99.93% of 95M rows in the 125B MoE Qwen3.8-Flash-Next). On Qwen3.8-Flash-Next routed experts with INT8 activations, KL to the FP8 model is 0.0557 vs 0.0553 for NVFP4 with scale search, and better than MLX MXFP4 and affine INT4; fully quantized, GSM8K is 94.8% vs 91.0% for MLX MXFP4. Prefill on M5 is 1.34–1.74x faster than MLX on a dense model, 1.10–1.57x on two MoEs up to 35B, and 1.22–1.29x on Qwen3.8-Flash-Next.

**Critique:** Only Apple M5 is benchmarked; the INT8-only premise matters less on GPUs with native FP4 tensor cores. Accuracy is mostly perplexity/KL on small models plus one GSM8K number; no broad downstream suite. Decode speed is not reported in the abstract, and the gain shrinks where non-GEMM work dominates. Read from the arXiv listing abstract only (abstract page was rate-limited).

### 3. [RankLane: Efficient MoE Prefill on Accelerators with Ranked Expert Capacities](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00421)

arXiv · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.00421
_Wentao Ni, Chihao Yu, Hong Cai, Mingu Lee, Hejia Zhang, Fatih Porikli et al._

**In one line:** RankLane fits MoE routing into static accelerator plans via load-ranked lanes; 1.27–4.18x prefill on Qwen3-30B-A3B vs dense

**What:** Accelerators with static execution plans must fix expert capacities before routing reveals expert load, so uniform worst-case capacity wastes compute on padding and smaller capacities overflow. RankLane ranks each device's experts by current load and assigns them to fixed-capacity tiers ('lanes'); runtime indices choose which experts and token rows fill each lane while shapes and placement stay fixed. An aggregation graph reduces each token's local expert contributions on one core, tier sizes are chosen from routing calibration and measured costs, and overflowing chunks are rerun at full capacity.

**Result:** Full-model prefill of Qwen3-30B-A3B and Gemma 4 26B-A4B with MXFP6 weights on four Qualcomm Cloud AI 100 Ultra SoCs, 128–2048-token chunks: 1.27–4.18x (Qwen3) and 1.93–3.46x (Gemma) end-to-end speedup over a dense baseline, including overflow detection and recovery.

**Critique:** The baseline is dense (all-expert or worst-case-capacity) execution, not other static-shape MoE schemes such as capacity-factor dropping or grouped-GEMM ports, so the margin over the best alternative is unclear. Single hardware platform; prefill only, no decode. Overflow rate and its cost on out-of-distribution routing are not given in the abstract. Read from the arXiv listing abstract only.

### 4. [MXCode: Vector Quantization for MX-Native Inference at Scale](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00403)

arXiv · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.00403
_Gaurav Kolhe, Cansu Demirkiran, Shubhi Tyagi, Sulaiman Sadiq, Boyao Wang, Richard Wang et al._

**In one line:** MXCode makes ~2-bit vector-quantized weights decode to MX-native FP4 operands; ~50% less load memory than FP4 on Qwen3-235B

**What:** Vector quantization can store weights at about 2 bits, but its codebooks usually don't map onto native FP4 compute. MXCode bridges them with three strategies: PostScale quantizes learned codebooks to MXFP4; PreScale extracts MX scales before codebook learning; PreScale+ further vector-quantizes those scales. All reconstruct MX-native W4A4 operands without materializing high-precision intermediate weights, trading quality against scale overhead and codebook granularity.

**Result:** Evaluated on Qwen3 (235B), DeepSeek-R1 (671B) and GLM-5.2 (753B) via the Microsoft MX Emulation Library: about 2 bits/weight with 'competitive, workload-dependent' quality. On GB300 with Qwen3-235B, about 50% less model-load memory than native FP4 at near-FP4 serving throughput; up to 94.5% better P90 time-to-first-token under memory-constrained prefix reuse.

**Critique:** Quality is described only as 'competitive' and 'workload-dependent' in the abstract, with no accuracy deltas versus FP4 or versus other 2-bit methods (e.g. QuIP#, AQLM, QTIP). Accuracy runs use an emulation library; the 94.5% TTFT gain comes from a memory-constrained scenario chosen to favor compression. Read from the arXiv listing abstract only.

### 5. [TVP: TV is Almost All You Need for Speculative Decoding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00588)

arXiv · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.00588
_Aaryam Sharma_

**In one line:** TVP trains speculative drafters on an acceptance-length loss built on total variation; +9.8% acceptance length vs cross-entropy

**What:** Under lossless speculative sampling a draft token is accepted with probability 1 − TV(target, draft), yet drafters are trained with cross-entropy or KL. The paper finds plain TV matches or beats published draft objectives when fine-tuning (and a fixed TV+KL mix does so from scratch), then fixes two issues: TV's vanishing gradient on tokens the drafter rarely covers, and prefix products in acceptance-length objectives starving later draft positions. TVP is a loss family derived from expected acceptance length whose power parameter moves from a coverage-oriented to an acceptance-oriented objective; it is annealed from 0 to 1.

**Result:** Highest or tied-highest acceptance length across three drafter architectures and three target models, including longer drafts and block verification; +9.8% acceptance length over cross-entropy when fine-tuning and +6.2% to +12.9% over the public DFlash2 training recipes.

**Critique:** Reports acceptance length, not end-to-end wall-clock speedup. Single-author paper; target model sizes and compute are not given in the abstract. Gains over DFlash2 compare against its public recipes, which may not be the authors' tuned settings. Read from the arXiv listing abstract only.

### 6. [Library of Layers (LoL): Adaptively Ordering Transformer Layers at Runtime](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.03526)

HF Daily Papers · paper · architecture · arXiv:2611.03526
_Abdellah Rahmani, Sean McLeish, Ang Li, Sanae Lotfi, Pascal Frossard, Micah Goldblum_

**In one line:** Library of Layers routes tokens through a shared pool of transformer blocks in an input-dependent order, between dense, looped and MoE

**What:** Instead of a fixed layer stack, LoL keeps one global library of transformer blocks and a router chooses, at inference time, which block to run at each depth step. Its single hyperparameter, library size, moves the model between parameter reuse (depth-recurrent) and sparsity (MoE-like), spanning dense, looped and MoE designs. Code is public (github.com/arahmani1/library-of-layers).

**Result:** The abstract reports text pretraining 'competitive' with dense, depth-recurrent and MoE baselines; on synthetic sudoku and maze tasks different orderings correspond to different solution strategies and can be forced by editing routing; layer-order patterns differ by language/script; probes on router outputs separate safe from unsafe text with 'high AUROC'. No numbers are given on the page.

**Critique:** No quantitative results in the abstract: model scale, token budget, FLOPs matching and the size of the gap to baselines are all unstated, so 'competitive' cannot be checked without the paper. The efficiency case (compute per token, kernel friendliness of dynamic block order, batching under per-token routing) is not addressed on the page. Read from the HF page only.

### 7. [Guarded Compression of Adam Optimizer State with Shared-Gradient Bounds](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00589)

arXiv · paper · training · arXiv:2611.00589

**In one line:** Guarded 8-bit log codes cut Adam moment storage ~70% vs FP32 while certifying the first restored update within tolerance

**What:** Adam's two FP32 moments are twice the size of FP32 weights, and compressing them is risky because the update divides by sqrt(v). The method accepts a coordinate's compressed moments only if, for every gradient in a clipped domain, they reproduce the native FP32 Adam update within tolerance τ (in learning-rate units); other coordinates are stored exactly. Bounding each update separately fails, so they bound the difference of the two updates directly using the shared gradient and parameter, with FP32 rounding allowances and a fixed mixed linear-logarithmic gradient partition.

**Result:** On 1M OPT-125M coordinates, the naive certificate lets 98.9% escape (payload 126.6% of FP32); the shared-gradient bound cuts escapes to 0.53% and payload to 28.3% at τ=0.01. Over 120 checkpoint restores (OPT-125M, ResNet-18), guarded 8-bit log codes shrink moment payloads 69.5% / 71.9% vs FP32, 40.8% / 45.4% vs guarded BF16, and 29.3% / 37.6% vs guarded blockwise 8-bit; codec time is 2.3–2.6x guarded BF16.

**Critique:** Tiny models only (OPT-125M, ResNet-18). The guarantee covers only the first update after restore under a pinned Torch op model; later training is measured empirically. This targets checkpoint/storage size, not in-training memory like 8-bit Adam, and there is no comparison to unguarded 8-bit optimizers' training quality. Last author's name was truncated on the listing, so authors are omitted. Read from the arXiv listing abstract only.

### 8. [TileWeave: Heterogeneous Tiling for Sparse Prefill on Mobile SoCs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00408)

arXiv · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.00408
_Wentao Ni, Hong Cai, Mingu Lee, Chihao Yu, Zhongming Yu, Hejia Zhang et al._

**In one line:** TileWeave splits block-sparse attention between phone NPU and GPU by key reuse; 1.35–3.23x prefill vs XAttention on Snapdragon

**What:** Block-sparse attention cuts long-context prefill cost, but its irregular block choices clash with mobile NPUs that want large regular tiles. TileWeave runs key blocks shared by many queries as coarse NPU tiles and sends low-reuse blocks to the GPU only for the query blocks that selected them, with an NPU-native pooled-key block selector and a runtime that streams GPU partial results into the NPU's output store in consumption order.

**Result:** On RULER, matches XAttention accuracy with Qwen3-1.7B and improves on it with Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct; mean LongBench matches XAttention. On Snapdragon SM8750 and SM8850 phones, full-model prefill is 1.35–3.23x faster than XAttention over 1k–16k tokens and up to 1.58x faster than dense attention.

**Critique:** Speedup over dense attention (up to 1.58x) is much smaller than over XAttention, so XAttention's own mobile port may be a weak baseline. Small models (≤3B) and contexts ≤16k only; energy and decode are not reported in the abstract. The abstract calls the system 'HeteroSparse' in its results, an unresolved naming inconsistency. Read from the arXiv listing abstract only.

### 9. [Order-Only On-Policy Distillation](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.02112)

HF Daily Papers · paper · training · arXiv:2611.02112
_Guangzeng Han, Yu Wu, Xiaolei Huang_

**In one line:** O³PD distills only the teacher's top-K token ranking on student rollouts; beats standard on-policy distillation in 6 of 6 pairs

**What:** On-policy distillation matches the teacher's token probabilities on student-generated prefixes, but those probabilities mix preference with calibration/confidence the student may not be able to copy, and depend on teacher temperature. O³PD instead trains the student to reproduce the order of the teacher's top-K tokens at each prefix, ignoring probability values, which makes the objective temperature-invariant.

**Result:** Across six teacher–student pairs, O³PD beats OPD on the average of HMMT25, AIME24 and AIME25 in every pair, by up to 2.9 points; largest single gain 5.6 points on AIME25. Other ranking losses give similar gains.

**Critique:** Evaluated only on three small competition-math benchmarks where single-problem variance is high; no seeds or confidence intervals on the page. Model sizes and compute are not stated, and no code is listed. Read from the HF page only.

### 10. [Wave Forcing: Towards Speed-of-Light Streaming Video Generation](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.00859)

HF Daily Papers · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.00859
_Yijie Jin, Yuxuan Liu, Jiajun Xu, Ye Huang, Chuzhi Wang, Jiayang Li et al._

**In one line:** Wave Forcing pipelines staggered denoising across GPU replicas for streaming video; 134.8 end-to-end FPS on 8 H200s

**What:** Chunk-wise autoregressive video generators are slow because serialized denoising and history-cache refresh need two forward passes per chunk. Wave Forcing uses chunk-wise causal attention so later chunks can read earlier, partly denoised chunks, and spreads staggered denoising and cache-refresh stages across full-model replicas ('1F1C': one chunk out per concurrent forward round after pipeline fill). A three-stage training recipe (mixed-noise denoising, motion supervision, causal adaptation on self-generated history) adapts the model; the WaveRT runtime adds Wave Parallelism for early K/V delivery with comm/compute overlap, optimized kernels and pipelined VAE decoding. Code is public.

**Result:** On Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B, 5-step WF matches Rolling Forcing overall quality with higher Dynamic Degree. On 8x H200, 4-step WF reaches 147.6 DiT FPS and 134.8 end-to-end FPS (7.07x single-GPU end-to-end). In DiT-only tests with matched SageAttention+W8A8 backends, Wave Parallelism gives up to 2.53x the throughput of the faster of Ulysses and Ring context parallelism across 1.3B–14B models.

**Critique:** The 7.07x figure is throughput on 8 GPUs versus 1, not per-GPU efficiency; quality is shown only on a 1.3B model. Video generation is adjacent to the LLM focus, though the parallelism scheme is general. Read from the HF page only.

### 11. [Sparks of Weight-Space Programming: LLMs can natively read & write neural network weights](https://rikiyat.github.io/blog/weight-space-programming/)

MIT CSAIL authors' blog · news · other

**In one line:** MIT CSAIL post: GPT-6 Astra edits 0.016% of Qwen3.5-0.8B's weights, lifting MATH-500 from 7.9% to 48.1% without gradients

**What:** A blog post by Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang and Omar Khattab (MIT CSAIL, 2 Nov) reports that the GPT-6 Astra agent can read and write small networks' weights from natural-language goals. In the Qwen3.5-0.8B experiment Astra had a Python kernel over the weights (no GPU), GSM8K/MATH training problems and a budget of up to 50 forward passes; it traced reasoning loops to one MLP direction in layers 15 and 19 and removed it from 64 output columns per layer (131,072 weights). Other experiments: identifying what small game, diffusion and LM networks were trained on from weights, fixing a failing Kinetix controller, and writing MinAtar policies from zero weights. It reached the Hacker News front page alongside a widely upvoted rebuttal by Matthew Honnibal.

**Result:** Qwen3.5-0.8B with 50 forward passes: MATH-500 7.9%→48.1%, GSM8K 13.8%→67.1%, IFEval 21.0%→62.3%; share of MATH-500 answers hitting the 32,768-token limit 93.0%→33.1%. With 10 or 25 passes, MATH-500 only reached 12.3% and 14.1%. Gemma 3 270M could not be improved (6.8%→at most 7.7%).

**Critique:** Off focus (interpretability/agents), included for its prominence. The base model was failing mostly by looping into the token limit, so the gain is largely removing a failure mode, which the authors concede ('editing can remove an obstacle but cannot add a missing ability'). Honnibal's rebuttal (honnibal.dev, 3 Nov) notes only three experiments read raw weights without tools, the from-scratch MinAtar policies beat 500M-step PPO only on Breakout (517 vs 25) and lost on Seaquest (53 vs 89) and Asterix (53 vs 55), and the sycophancy and Gemma tasks failed. Not peer reviewed; GPT-6 Astra itself is not independently accessible for replication.
