### 1. [BeNice! Closing Looping's Inference-Memory Gap via KV-Sharing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31633)

arXiv · paper · inference · arXiv:2610.31633
_Hayden Prairie, Jacob M. Ryan, Taylor Berg-Kirkpatrick, Daniel Y. Fu_

**In one line:** BeNice! lets looped LMs share one KV cache across recurrences at matched perplexity; up to 83.6% lower p90 TTFT in simulated serving

**What:** Looped (recurrent-depth) transformers reuse the same blocks for several recurrences, so parameters stay fixed but the KV cache grows with every pass. The paper defines KV-shareability: whether the last recurrence's KV cache can stand in for the earlier ones. It uses two stages. Stochastic depth in pretraining (recurrence count sampled per sequence from a Poisson distribution) pushes the residual stream toward a shared limit across recurrences. BeNice!, a post-training stage, then broadcasts the final recurrence's KV cache into the first recurrence and trains the model to stay compatible with it. The authors trained Parcae models from scratch (140M to 1.3B, over 100B ClimbMix tokens) and also tested released Huginn-3.5B and Ouro checkpoints. They report that KV-sharing combines with memory-efficient sequence mixers and low-precision KV quantization.

**Result:** Validation perplexity on fineweb-edu with a shared cache: 770M goes from 21.61 (standard finetuning) to 11.86 with BeNice! (unshared: 11.85); 1.3B goes from 18.52 to 10.78 (unshared: 10.79). Against unshared looping in sglang, replaying Mooncake traces: cache hit rate up to +31.4% (8.1 points), throughput up to +23.6%, and p90 TTFT down from 139.2 s to 23.2 s (-83.6%). At 1.3B, batch 64 and 2,048-token context, HBM use drops from 63.82 GB to 21.60 GB.

**Critique:** The serving gains come from proxy models (GLM-4.7-FP8 with its middle third of blocks looped) under simulated low-memory traffic, not a trained looped model in production. Stochastic depth alone does not guarantee shareability (the 770M and 1.3B Parcae models fail it without BeNice!), so an extra post-training stage is needed. Downstream task accuracy, beyond perplexity, is not reported in the parts read. The text gives the request rate as 22 req/s in one place and 2 req/s in another. The KV-quantization combination uses only basic methods. No code link was found.

### 2. [Is 2:4 Sparsity Still Worth It? Semi-Structured Sparsity versus Quantization in LLM Inference](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.28947)

arXiv · paper · efficiency · arXiv:2610.28947
_Xiuyi Qin_

**In one line:** On L40S/H100/Blackwell, 2:4 sparsity loses to the next lower precision in error and speed; it helps only at the FP8/NVFP4 floor

**What:** The paper asks a practical question: at a given precision, should you add 2:4 semi-structured sparsity or drop to the next lower bit-width? It compares magnitude, Wanda and SparseGPT 2:4 pruning against RTN, GPTQ and AWQ quantization (FP8, INT3-INT8 with group scales, MXFP4, NVFP4). It covers six operand families per layer (weights, linear inputs, Q, K, V and attention probabilities), on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen3-8B. Kernels were timed with CUPTI on L40S, H100 and RTX PRO 6000 (Blackwell). The author also derives a parameter-free predictor, the '2:4 energy loss': the share of tensor energy held by the two entries dropped from each group of four.

**Result:** At equal bits (the 2:4 index counted), 2:4 lost at every storage budget, by more than 10x in error at 8 bits. At the 2x tensor-core tier, 2:4+FP16 had about 5-16x the error of dense FP8 and ran slower (0.74x on H100). Stacked on FP8, 2:4 sped up linear layers 1.3-1.7x. In vLLM on one H100, the retrained Sparse-Llama-3.1-8B-2of4 in FP8 reached 74.6k vs 58.9k prefill tok/s and 234 vs 198 decode tok/s at batch 1 against dense FP8. One-shot 2:4 on FP8 collapsed GSM8K to 0.028 (dense: 0.552). The energy-loss criterion ranks operator-level outcomes with correlation 0.93 (AUC 0.95), while global kurtosis does poorly.

**Critique:** Single-author preprint covering only two 8B models; the quantization-floor experiments use only Llama-3.1-8B. The author's own retraining used 1B tokens against 13B for Sparse-Llama, so the poor Blackwell 4:8 result (GSM8K 0.132 vs 0.460 dense NVFP4) may reflect the budget rather than the pattern. GSM8K uses 250 problems (about +/-6 points at 95%), without chat templates and with Qwen3 thinking off. Operator-level errors ignore how errors compound across layers. The 4-bit weight floor was not timed.

### 3. [Evaluating the Applicability of LLM Inference Optimizations on Apple Silicon](https://research.macpaw.com/publications/llm-optimizations-apple-silicon/Evaluating_the_Applicability_of_LLM_Inference_Optimizations_on_Apple_Silicon__NeurIPS_ODI_2026.pdf)

MacPaw Research (arXiv 2610.31479) · paper · inference · arXiv:2610.31479
_Kyrylo Yemets, Andrii Yaroshevych, Victor Muryn, Ostap Khomenko, Pavlo Kryven, Maksym Kmet et al._

**In one line:** On M2/M3/M4 Macs at batch 1, no speculative decoding setup sped up 4-bit Qwen3-4B end to end; KV pruning gave up to 1.75x

**What:** Most LLM inference optimizations were validated on NVIDIA server GPUs under batched serving. This paper retests them for single-request decoding on Apple Silicon (M2 Pro, M3 Ultra and M4 Pro, with an RTX 4090 as control) using MLX/MLX-LM, llama.cpp GGUF, KVPress and llm-compressor. It covers KV-cache pruning (SnapKV, ExpectedAttention, FastKVzip and others) and prefix reuse; six speculative-decoding methods (classic, n-gram, cascade n-gram, EAGLE-3, DFlash, DDTree); Metal ports of a Marlin-style W4A16 GEMM, FlashInfer-style split-KV attention and an MoE gather kernel; and post-training quantization formats. Models include Qwen3-4B/8B, Gemma-3-4B, Granite-4.0-H-Micro and the LFM2.5 family. Each mechanism is labeled as transferable, a hardware gap, an overhead the runtime already handles, or not applicable at batch 1.

**Result:** Speculative decoding with a 4-bit Qwen3-4B target cut end-to-end throughput by 25.2-69.8% on M2 Pro and 8.9-61.7% on M3 Ultra, despite 17.1-80.5% acceptance; the one sizeable gain was DDTree on M3 Pro with a BF16 target (+17.8%). ExpectedAttention KV pruning gave 1.61-1.75x on the Macs at 4,096-token context versus 0.98x on the RTX 4090. The Marlin-style kernel gave 0.95-1.00x at batch 1 but 1.09-1.20x at M=2-8. MoE gather gave 1.03-1.12x everywhere. A fused megakernel ran 1.8x slower than stock MLX (dispatch overhead 3.04 us per boundary on MLX vs 9.91 us per CUDA launch). Q4_0 GGUF decode was +63.7% over 8-bit on M2 Pro.

**Critique:** All results are batch-1 single-request decoding; multi-request serving is not covered. KV pruning was not evaluated for task quality, so the speedups say nothing about accuracy loss. Speculative decoding used MLX adaptations, small mostly 4-bit targets and one chat workload, so the negative result may not hold for other implementations. The megakernel study covers one model on one chip. Some RTX comparisons use different stacks, so hardware-only attribution is limited. The authors label it a NeurIPS ODI 2026 workshop paper; it is not peer-reviewed at main-track level.

### 4. [Meta Expert: Cross-Layer Expert Sharing for Storage- and Retrieval-Efficient MoE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27598)

arXiv · paper · architecture · arXiv:2610.27598
_Ermo Hua, Xiangyu Hong, Baiting Wu, Che Jiang, Haozheng Hou, Yicheng Gu et al._

**In one line:** Meta Expert replaces per-layer MoE experts with a pool shared across layers; matches vanilla MoE using 70-80% as many experts

**What:** Standard MoE models give every layer its own set of 'local' experts, which the authors argue store redundant content across depth. Meta Expert replaces them with one pool of experts that every layer can route into, so experts are shared across layers. The paper also argues, theoretically, that sharing gives hidden states more flexible residual pathways for retrieving information. Per AI Weekly coverage (not the abstract), experiments use a depth ladder of 12-24 layers with 128 routed experts per layer and top-8 routing, trained on 200B tokens at 6B total / 1.6B active parameters, evaluated on MMLU, C-Eval, CMMLU, BBH, OpenBookQA and TriviaQA.

**Result:** Per the abstract, with activated-expert count and expert granularity held fixed, Meta Expert matches vanilla MoE using only 70-80% of the experts, with lower parameter redundancy, lower cross-entropy loss and higher downstream accuracy than local-expert layouts. No per-benchmark numbers were available from the pages read.

**Critique:** Only the abstract and secondary coverage (AI Weekly) were read; arXiv rate-limited the full text. Per that coverage, the only baselines are the authors' own vanilla and local-expert MoE configurations at a single 6B-A1.6B scale, with no comparison to other cross-layer sharing or parameter-reuse schemes. Fewer stored experts does not by itself cut active compute or memory traffic per token, and the abstract gives no serving or offloading measurements to support the 'retrieval-efficient' claim. No code link.

### 5. [Extreme Compression of Per-Layer Embeddings via Cross-Layer Latent Vector Quantization (OPTIC)](https://github.com/VILA-Lab/optic)

GitHub (VILA-Lab/optic) · paper · efficiency · arXiv:2610.31309
_Jiacheng Liu, Yaxin Luo, Xiaohan Zhao, Peng Sun, Xinyi Shang, Jiacheng Cui et al._

**In one line:** OPTIC compresses Gemma 4 E4B's per-layer embedding tables ~100x while keeping 97.8% of BF16 performance, vs 66.3% for product quantization

**What:** Gemma 4 E2B and E4B give each token a separate per-layer embedding (PLE) vector at every decoder layer; in E4B the PLE tables hold 35.2% of parameters (5.64 GB in BF16). OPTIC (Output-Path-fitted, Token-shared Indexed Codes) stores one quantized code tuple per token, shared across all layers, and reconstructs each layer's vector with a small per-layer affine decoder. Codes and decoders are fitted to minimize error at the PLE output projection rather than on the raw table. Code (Apache-2.0) and a 100x artifact for BF16 Gemma 4 E4B are released.

**Result:** At about 100x, OPTIC retains 97.8% of BF16 E4B performance (geometric mean over seven benchmarks) vs 66.3% for product quantization; on the official W4A16 E4B release, 96.9% vs 82.4%. On E2B at about 100x, 89.1% vs 22.9% for PQ. At 1002x (5.6 MB of PLE) it retains 84.1% on E4B. Serving from the 100x codes on one RTX 4090 cuts GPU memory by 29.7% (BF16) and 43.1% (W4A16) with at most 0.69% added wall time.

**Critique:** The method targets one architectural feature (PLE tables), so it applies only to models that have them, here the Gemma 4 E-series. The only named baseline on the page read is product quantization; residual/additive VQ or low-rank factorizations of the tables are not shown there. The 'retention' metric is a geometric mean over seven tasks, which hides per-task drops. The E4B results are the authors' own; not independently reproduced. Details come from the repository; the arXiv page (2610.31309) could not be read in this run.

### 6. [SQuAT: Spherical Lattice Vector Quantization-Aware Training](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31412)

arXiv · paper · efficiency · arXiv:2610.31412
_Sebastian Loeschcke, Ivan Koryakovskiy, Tycho F. A. van der Ouderaa, Riccardo Del Chiaro, Mart van Baalen, Paul N. Whatmough et al._

**In one line:** SQuAT: VQ-aware training with E8 spherical-codebook directions plus scalar gains; distributed assignment cuts training time 12.8x

**What:** Lattice vector quantization gives good rate-distortion trade-offs for low-bit LLMs, but its behavior under quantization-aware training is poorly understood and hard to scale. SQuAT uses a shape-gain decomposition: each weight vector is split into a direction, quantized with an E8-derived spherical codebook, and a magnitude, quantized with a low-bit scalar quantizer. Shape and gain get separate straight-through estimators, which the authors argue induces tangent-space updates on the sphere, an implicit form of Riemannian optimization that a single STE over the whole quantizer ignores. A distributed exact nearest-neighbor assignment has workers quantize disjoint weight blocks and exchange only discrete indices, enabling VQ-QAT on 10B tokens. Models: instruction-tuned Llama-3.2-1B/3B and Qwen3-4B, and base Qwen3-1.7B.

**Result:** Per the abstract, SQuAT consistently outperforms standard VQ-QAT with a single STE under identical quantizers and initialization and reaches state-of-the-art VQ-QAT accuracy; the distributed assignment cuts VQ-QAT training time by 12.8x. The abstract gives no accuracy numbers or bit-widths.

**Critique:** Only the abstract was read; arXiv rate-limited the full text, so bit-widths, accuracy deltas and comparisons with other low-bit methods (e.g. QTIP, AQLM, ParetoQ or other E8-lattice QAT such as E8-QAT, arXiv 2610.27699) could not be checked. The only named baseline is standard VQ-QAT STE. Models top out at 4B parameters. Inference kernels and decode speed for the spherical codebook are not mentioned in the abstract. No code link.

### 7. [Rethinking Sequence Compression with Test-time Regression](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.31574)

HF Daily Papers · paper · architecture · arXiv:2610.31574
_Sihan Yang, Jie Huang, Hanxuan Li, Yifan Guo, Weigao Sun, Yiran Zhong et al._

**In one line:** TTR-Compressor compresses token blocks into closed-form regression coefficients; matches an MLP visual merger with 55.6% fewer visual tokens

**What:** Many efficient language and multimodal models compress sequences (block summaries for sparse attention, visual-token merging), usually by pooling or a learned projection. The paper recasts compression as test-time regression: the input tokens define a regression problem, and its fitted coefficients, computed in closed form during the forward pass, are the compressed representation. The regression is learned end to end by backpropagation. The resulting TTR-Compressor is tested as the block compressor in block-wise sparse attention and as the visual-token merger in multimodal LLMs. Code is on GitHub (Sphere-AI-Lab/ttr-compressor).

**Result:** Per the abstract, in block-wise sparse attention it substantially improves RULER scores over attention-based compressors, and in multimodal LLMs it consistently beats the standard MLP merger, matching its performance with 55.6% fewer visual tokens. Exact RULER numbers and base models were not available from the page read.

**Critique:** Only the Hugging Face abstract was read; arXiv rate-limited the full text, so model sizes, baselines beyond 'attention-based compressors' and 'MLP merger', and RULER numbers could not be checked. 'Substantially improves' is not quantified in the abstract. The cost of the closed-form solve (it implies a matrix inverse or similar per block) versus pooling is not addressed there, which matters for the efficiency claim.

### 8. [Cola2: Co-Adaptive Hierarchical Language Modeling with Continuous Semantic Compression and Balanced Discrete Generation](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.31305)

HF Daily Papers · paper · architecture · arXiv:2610.31305
_Hongcan Guo, Yian Zhao, Feng Wang, Shen Nie, Guoqiang Wei, Yan Zeng et al._

**In one line:** ByteDance Seed's Cola2 decodes continuous span latents with block-causal diffusion; beats Llama-3-8B on HellaSwag and StoryCloze

**What:** Cola2 is a hierarchical language model that separates continuous semantic modeling from discrete text output. Text is split dynamically into semantic spans whose compression rate is held by a negative-feedback mechanism with no auxiliary loss. An encoder learns compact latents from combined reconstruction and generation supervision. A Flow Matching prior models the latent sequence (separated from representation learning by stop-gradient), and a block-causal masked-diffusion decoder turns latents into tokens. The decoder is trained on clean, noisy and dropped latent conditions, with classifier-free guidance balancing reconstruction against free generation. Token-prediction gradients flow back into the encoder through the latents. At inference, generated text is re-encoded under a fixed span layout so the latent history matches the text.

**Result:** Per the abstract, design choices are validated on five downstream tasks at about 210B training tokens, with scaling that has not saturated. An unconverged checkpoint trained on 346.03B source tokens averages 60.10 over eight tasks and beats Llama-3-8B on HellaSwag and StoryCloze, despite Llama-3-8B's reported pretraining budget being over 43x larger.

**Critique:** Only the Hugging Face abstract was read. The headline comparison picks two of eight tasks against Llama-3-8B, an older model, and compares tokens rather than compute; per-task results on the other six are not given there. Generation speed or FLOPs per token, the main reason for latent hierarchical generation, is not quantified in the abstract. The 8B model is not yet released (the submitter says 'in the coming weeks'), and no code is linked.

### 9. [Deep Fusion Memory: A Chunk-Level Parametric Memory for Large Language Models](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.25100)

HF Daily Papers · paper · architecture · arXiv:2610.25100
_Rubin Wei, Siyuan Sun, Hao Sun, Yuliang Liu, Xiang Shi, Jiarui Wang et al._

**In one line:** Deep Fusion Memory adds chunk-level predicted memory to a frozen LLM via gated cross-attention, no retrieval at inference

**What:** To add external knowledge without continued pretraining (forgetting) or inference-time retrieval (latency), DFM attaches a parametric memory module to a frozen backbone. A causal memory predictor generates a memory vector from the preceding context, supervised by retrieved chunk embeddings from the corpus. One predicted memory is shared by all tokens in a chunk and fused into intermediate hidden states through gated cross-attention. Predictor and fusion are trained end to end; inference needs no explicit retrieval. Building targets per chunk rather than per token shrinks the offline retrieval workload.

**Result:** Per the abstract, DFM needs 32x fewer retrieval queries and 2048x fewer retrieved items to build its training data than token-level parametric memory methods. Across six QA tasks it beats the strongest baseline by 12.71% on Mistral-7B-v0.3 and 11.19% on Qwen3-8B-Base; on domain adaptation it adds 6.51 points over Qwen3-4B-Base and 4.38 over Qwen3-8B-Base.

**Critique:** Only the Hugging Face abstract was read; the strongest baseline is not named there, and it is unclear whether the 12.71% is relative or absolute. Inference cost of the extra cross-attention and memory predictor is not reported in the abstract, nor is a comparison with plain RAG at equal latency. No code or models are linked. Published on arXiv 18 Oct; surfaced on HF Daily Papers on 23 Oct.

### 10. [A Fix for Softmax and Cross-Entropy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31642)

arXiv · paper · training · arXiv:2610.31642
_Jonathan T. Barron, Dan Gnanapragasam, Michael Tschannen_

**In one line:** Barron et al. identify two numerical bugs in standard softmax/cross-entropy and give fixed implementations that remove the Slingshot effect

**What:** The paper identifies two numerical failure modes in standard log-softmax, softmax and cross-entropy implementations. 'Softmax Collapse': when the target logit is much larger than the others, its gradient is zeroed and gradients no longer sum to zero. 'Softmax Cancellation': when student and teacher logits are nearly equal, the computed gradient is nonzero when it should be zero. The authors give simple replacement implementations that fix both.

**Result:** The abstract reports qualitative effects only: the fix eliminates the 'Slingshot' phenomenon, speeds up grokking with weight decay, prevents accuracy collapse without weight decay, and stops students drifting from teachers in distillation. It also shows that after memorization, logit growth is bounded by an equilibrium between weight decay and Adam's epsilon. No quantitative numbers or baselines beyond 'standard implementations' are given in the abstract.

**Critique:** Only the abstract was read. The evidence described is from grokking-style and distillation settings; whether the bugs measurably affect large-scale LLM pretraining in bf16/fp32 is not shown in the abstract. No code link on the abstract page. Included as off-focus because it touches a component every model trains through.
