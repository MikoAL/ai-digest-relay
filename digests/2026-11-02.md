### 1. [Muon Makes the First Mixture-of-Experts Layer Harder to Balance](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37914)

arXiv · paper · training · arXiv:2610.37914
_Marcos Hernanz_

**In one line:** Muon-trained MoE LMs: first MoE layer's busiest expert gets 4–7x fair load after warmup; mean-centering router inputs removes it

**What:** Many open MoE LMs train with Muon and balance experts with a per-expert bias on routing scores that is updated after each step (auxiliary-loss-free balancing). The paper tracks the bias span (max minus min in a layer) as a measure of how hard a layer is to balance, on models with 55M and 108M active parameters. Using Muon on the rest of the network, whatever optimizer trains the router, makes the first MoE layer's router inputs point in a shared direction. That concentrates tokens on one expert. Two fixes are tested: subtracting the batch mean from the first router's inputs during the first 3,000 steps, and halving Muon's learning rate. Code and runs are on GitHub (marcoshernanz/muon-moe-routing).

**Result:** With learning rates tuned on shorter runs, Muon roughly doubled the first MoE layer's bias span on average, in every seed. Other layers changed much less. Shortly after warmup, the busiest first-layer expert received 4–7x its fair share of tokens. Mean-centering the router inputs removed the overload with little effect on loss. On the smaller model, halving Muon's LR removed most of the span increase and slightly improved loss. Moving only the router to AdamW made loads shift more during warmup and slightly raised loss.

**Critique:** Tiny scale (55M/108M active) and a single author (the repo says it comes from a bachelor's thesis). The abstract gives no loss values or expert counts, and the effect may not hold at the billion-parameter scale of the production recipes it cites. The only comparisons are Muon vs. AdamW and the two mitigations. Other balancing schemes (auxiliary loss, capacity limits) and other Muon variants are not tested. The results are not yet independently reproduced.

### 2. [Multi-Draft On-Policy Distillation for Diffusion Drafters](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37778)

arXiv · paper · inference · arXiv:2610.37778
_Petr Molodyk, Yuchen Zhu, Jaemoo Choi, Molei Tao, Yongxin Chen_

**In one line:** MDraft pools verifier targets over several sampled draft blocks to train diffusion drafters; DFlash acceptance length +11.9% at T=1

**What:** One-step diffusion drafters for speculative decoding (here DFlash) predict a whole block in parallel, so each drafted token is predicted without seeing the other tokens in its block. The verifier does condition on them. In on-policy distillation, different sampled prefixes therefore give conflicting targets for the same drafter prediction, which suppresses tokens that are plausible under some prefixes. MDraft samples K draft blocks from the same context and pools the verifier's full-vocabulary predictions across those prefixes, mixed with weight α. It uses a reverse-KL loss with survival-position weighting. Only training changes: the drafter's architecture and inference stay the same, and it replaces the pretrained checkpoint directly.

**Result:** With a Qwen3-4B verifier and a DFlash drafter (z-lab/Qwen3-4B-DFlash-b16, 15 drafted tokens), mean acceptance length went 6.432→6.685 at T=0 (+3.9%), 5.892→6.321 at T=0.6 (+7.3%) and 5.274→5.903 at T=1 (+11.9%). AdaFlash reached 6.671 / 6.168 / 5.546 and Draft-OPD 6.347 / 6.041 / 5.552. With Qwen3-8B, MDraft got 5.748 vs. 5.522 for AdaFlash at T=1, but slightly trailed it at T=0 (6.631 vs. 6.655). Averages are over GSM8K, MATH500, AIME25, MBPP, HumanEval and MT-Bench.

**Critique:** No wall-clock speedup is reported; acceptance length stands in for it. Gains at greedy decoding are small (about 0.25 tokens) and within reach of AdaFlash. All results come from one evaluation seed, with a 2,048-token cap and thinking disabled, which favours short outputs over long reasoning traces. Draft-OPD was reproduced with random anchors rather than live rollouts. The 8B run uses different α and K, so the two scales are not matched. Only one drafter family is tested.

### 3. [What a Weight Update Buys: Leverage Across Training Stages](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37719)

arXiv · paper · training · arXiv:2610.37719
_Shikhar Shiromani_

**In one line:** Fisher 'leverage' metric: pretraining updates ≈ random on prose; bf16 training without fp32 master weights freezes 36–99% of coords

**What:** The paper defines the leverage of a weight update as its Fisher quadratic form divided by the same quantity for random directions of equal norm at the same checkpoint. It is computed with one forward-mode pass per direction. Leverage is measured across released Olmo-3, Tülu-3, Zephyr and OLMo-2 checkpoints and their training stages, and in controlled SFT/DPO runs. The efficiency-relevant part studies what happens when bf16 parameters are trained without an fp32 master copy. A sign-flip null model is also used to predict how much DARE (random drop-and-rescale) damages a model at keep rate p.

**Result:** Leverage spans four orders of magnitude: pretraining windows of 30k–1M steps sit at ≈1 (the random baseline) on prose, SFT at about 5x, RL-Zero/RLVR at 400–1,500 and DPO at 400–23,000. Training bf16 parameters with no fp32 master copy freezes 36–99% of coordinates and cuts leverage by up to 7x. The DARE prediction (1−p)/(p·ρ_sign) is within 2x of measured damage wherever DARE works. Two re-runs of a released DPO stage land within 1% of its weight displacement and 4% of its leverage.

**Critique:** A diagnostic study, not a training method, by a single author. The bf16-freezing finding fits the known stochastic-rounding/master-weight problem, but the abstract does not test mitigations such as stochastic rounding or Kahan summation. Results mostly come from released checkpoints, where data and recipes vary together, plus small controlled runs whose model sizes the abstract does not state. 'Leverage on prose' depends on the evaluation distribution chosen. No code is linked.

### 4. [Improving Data-Constrained Scaling with Hyperparameter Transfer and Ensembling](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37842)

arXiv · paper · scaling · arXiv:2610.37842
_Ethan Baron, Shmuel Berman, Anirudh Satheesh, Akshay Vegesna, Samip Dahal, Andrew Gordon Wilson_

**In one line:** Data-constrained scaling recipe with weight-decay transfer and ensembling: >0.15 nats lower loss on 100M FineWeb tokens

**What:** Prior guidance on data-constrained scaling (training for many epochs on a fixed dataset) keeps hyperparameters such as weight decay fixed, although their best values shift with model size and epoch count. It also ignores ensembling. The authors argue this can make loss rise as compute grows. They fit power laws that predict how to transfer hyperparameters across model size and epochs, plus scaling laws for how much compute to put into training several ensemble members rather than one larger or longer-trained model.

**Result:** On a 100M-token FineWeb budget, the recipe lowers validation loss by more than 0.15 nats (about 14% lower perplexity) compared with prior data-constrained recipes. Ensembling gives monotonic gains as compute grows. Prescriptions fit on smaller runs held up at compute budgets up to 12x larger.

**Critique:** The abstract does not name the prior recipes it beats, or give model sizes or absolute losses. 100M tokens is a very small data budget, and 12x extrapolation is modest next to frontier scales. Ensembles also multiply inference cost, which a loss-per-training-FLOP comparison hides. Downstream task results are not mentioned. No code is linked.

### 5. [Think Twice: The Metis-1.6 Technical Report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37903)

arXiv · paper · architecture · arXiv:2610.37903
_Giulianno Vollmer_

**In one line:** Metis-1.6: one-author hybrid Mamba-2/attention-MoE LMs run twice per token; 10.2B-A1.18B Logos averages 52.93 on 40 benchmarks

**What:** Two models built from scratch by one researcher: Praxis (2.94B total, 0.46B active) and Logos (10.23B total, 1.18B active). Both use a hybrid stack of Mamba-2 and attention mixture-of-experts layers, and each token passes through the stack twice (a form of looping). Training used 1.02T tokens: 804.76B pretraining, 18B context extension to 131,072 tokens, and 200B SFT. The report documents failures in detail: an optimizer misconfiguration that never ran (attention trained with AdamW throughout pretraining), a 1.77B-parameter n-gram memory that switched itself off, five SFT choices that hurt capability, and a length-reward sign inversion under group-mean normalization. That inversion penalized long correct solutions once a group's pass rate exceeded 2/3; the author proves it and fixes it with a one-line clamp. The report ends with 10B-token ablations of MoRE (Mixture of Recursive Experts), the looped design planned for Metis-1.7. Weights are on Hugging Face (Lernex).

**Result:** Logos averages 52.93 over 40 post-trained benchmarks, 7th of 17 models in the author's comparison set and within one point of Qwen3-4B. The author says this uses about one-twelfth of the median training data of comparison models that disclose theirs. On math it ranks 2nd of 30, with 67.50% on AIME 2025 and 63.64% on HMMT Feb 2026. It is weak on code, instruction following and long context.

**Critique:** The benchmark suite, comparison set and harness are the author's own, and no results are independently reproduced. The comparison is mainly to Qwen3-4B, a dense model of different vintage. The abstract reports no training compute. Because of the documented misconfigurations, the architecture's contribution cannot be separated from the training bugs, and running the stack twice per token is not ablated against a non-looped baseline at equal compute. The MoRE ablations are only 10B tokens.

### 6. [Turing LLM Post-Training: Calibrated Distillation and Efficient Dynamic-MoE Execution](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39876)

arXiv · paper · training · arXiv:2610.39876

**In one line:** C-MOPD merges domain specialists into a 20B-A2B dynamic MoE via calibrated multi-teacher on-policy distillation; 2.86x faster step

**What:** The paper targets post-training of a dynamically routed MoE (Turing-20B-A2B), where separately optimized specialist models are consolidated into one student. The method, C-MOPD (calibrated multi-teacher on-policy distillation), aligns the student with each selected teacher over a local Top-K vocabulary set, using per-teacher loss normalization and clipping. On the systems side, the execution pipeline uses bounded-width expert dispatch after capacity limits, replays the final routes, and overlaps rollout with training.

**Result:** The selected model reaches a normalized integration score of 0.9988 across four domains and keeps at least 90.16% of each specialist's measured gain. At K=32 the full calibrated objective scores 0.9962, against 0.9724 without calibration and 0.9834 without clipping. Under an 8K response cap, its mean on four math/code reasoning benchmarks is 85.92, vs. 80.33 for Qwen3-8B and 74.21 for shared SFT. It beats SFT on 10 of 12 broader metrics. On PPU hardware, the execution changes cut the consolidation step from 398.7 s to 139.3 s (2.86x vs. serial execution).

**Critique:** The integration score is defined by the authors, and the abstract does not name the four reasoning benchmarks. Qwen3-8B is a dense model with 4x the active parameters, which is a loose comparison. The baselines are SFT and the method's own ablations; other multi-teacher or model-merging methods are not compared. The speedup is measured against serial execution on proprietary PPU hardware and may not carry over to GPUs. No code or weights are linked.

### 7. [PyTorch 2.15 Release Blog](https://pytorch.org/blog/pytorch-2-15-release-blog/)

PyTorch blog · release · efficiency

**In one line:** PyTorch 2.15: rewritten NCCL backend by default, FSDP2 fp32 overrides for router/norm params, NVFP4 decode fusion on Blackwell

**What:** Released 28 Oct. Distributed: a rewritten NCCL backend (ported from torchcomms) is now the default for nccl process groups. Communicators are created eagerly, there is a shrink/grow fast path (NCCL 2.29+), and the old backend is available as 'nccl-legacy'. FSDP2 MixedPrecisionPolicy gains param_dtype_override_fn, which keeps precision-sensitive parameters such as MoE router gates and norm weights in fp32 while the rest trains in bf16. A full gradient-accumulation step can be captured in one CUDA graph. Pipeline parallelism becomes cudagraphable and uses less memory. Kernels: on Blackwell, the NVFP4 dequant scale of ModelOpt NVFP4 layers is folded into the NVGEMM CuTeDSL GEMM, removing a pointwise launch per layer during decode. Inductor autotunes FlyDSL templates (GEMM, ragged grouped_mm, MXFP4/MXFP8 scaled_mm) on AMD gfx950. Apple MPS gets FP8 scaled_mm and gemv decode paths, and Python 3.15 is supported. Most of these features are tagged 'API Unstable'.

**Result:** The only speedups reported are for Apple MPS: F.linear decode 1.23x geometric mean (up to 5x on small projections), MoE grouped_mm decode 1.32–1.34x tokens/s on M3 Ultra, and single-row softmax over a 128,256-entry vocabulary 8x faster.

**Critique:** There are no speedup numbers for the headline datacenter features (new NCCL backend, FSDP2 changes, NVFP4 decode fusion, FlyDSL on AMD), so their real-world effect is unverified. Most new APIs are marked unstable, and Rubin support is experimental. Making the rewritten NCCL backend the default is a behaviour change worth testing before upgrading production training jobs.

### 8. [ISTA-DASLab Ships 2-Bit DeepSeek V4.1 Flash at 100% Task Recovery](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/ista-daslab-ships-2-bit-deepseek-v41-flash-at-100-task-recovery)

AI Weekly · release · inference

**In one line:** Report: ISTA-DASLab releases GSQ/RCO 2-bit GGUF builds of DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, matching the reference on AIME25, GPQA-D and LCB v6

**What:** According to AI Weekly (28 Oct), quoting the Hugging Face card, ISTA-DASLab released GGUF builds of DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (763.2B-parameter sparse MoE, 384 routed experts, 6 active, 1M context) quantized with two methods. GSQ is post-training scalar quantization that jointly learns per-coordinate grid assignments and per-group scales through a Gumbel-Softmax relaxation. RCO picks one of K quantization types per tensor under a total size budget, using a Riemannian-manifold formulation that enforces the budget without extra tuning. Builds: IQ2_XXS (2.00 bpw, 248.84 GB), IQ2_XS (2.25 bpw) and IQ1_M (1.75 bpw, 231.32 GB). They need llama.cpp b11870 or newer. Via Strata they run on a 12 GB GPU, with the rest of the model in system RAM and 110.60 GB of Engram lookup tables memory-mapped from SSD.

**Result:** Per the article, the IQ2_XXS build scores 100.00 on AIME25, 90.91 on GPQA-Diamond and 89.71 on LiveCodeBench v6, the same as the FP8-dense/FP4-routed reference at reasoning effort 50. IQ2_XS keeps 99.8% and IQ1_M 98.7% of the reference task average.

**Critique:** This item rests on secondary coverage: the Hugging Face model card could not be fetched in this run. '100% recovery' covers only three tasks at one reasoning-effort setting. Scores like AIME25 = 100 leave no room to detect degradation, and perplexity or long-context results are not reported. Throughput with most weights in system RAM and the lookup tables on SSD is not given. The article dates from 28 Oct, so it is slightly older than this digest's 24-hour window.
