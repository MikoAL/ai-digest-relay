### 1. [SpecPilot: Autonomous Algorithm Discovery and Systems Optimization for Speculative Decoding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02501)

arXiv · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.02501
_Kunjun Li, Wenhao Chai, Yihao Liang, Yuezhou Hu, Shuyao Xu, Yi Pan et al._

**In one line:** SpecPilot: agent loop tunes speculative decoding per deployment; speedup over AR rises from 4.46x (stock) to 10.87x at batch 1

**What:** Released parallel-drafting systems fall short of the speedup parallel drafting allows: at batch 1 each step accepts few tokens but still reads every target weight, and with batching per-request verification roughly halves the gain over autoregressive decoding. The best fix also depends on serving regime, drafter, target and GPU. SpecPilot is an agentic search loop: each round a cost model ranks the largest gaps between the current system and its theoretical ceiling, parallel agents try directions and merge the ones that work, token forcing checks every output token against the stock system to catch reward hacking, and a structured memory carries validated mechanisms to later rounds and deployments. Mechanisms it found include a lossless 12-bit weight format, tree verification for hybrid recurrent targets, two-level verification and three batching mechanisms.

**Result:** Over 4 target models, 3 drafters, 2 GPU platforms and 5 benchmarks, with no training and no quality loss: speedup over AR goes from 4.46x (stock released systems) to 10.87x at one request, and from 1.94x to 3.93x with batching.

**Critique:** The baseline is the stock released systems, not hand-tuned expert implementations, so part of the gain may be engineering any careful team would do. The abstract does not say how much compute and agent time the search costs per deployment, or how the mechanisms transfer to unseen models. The 12-bit format and verification tricks are interesting on their own, but the abstract gives no per-mechanism ablation. No code link on the abstract page.

### 2. [The Gating Mechanism of KV Cache Eviction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07605)

arXiv · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.07605
_Hao Gu, Xintong Yang, Zhiwei Li, Lei Zhu, Jian Yang, Qiyuan Zhu et al._

**In one line:** Learned in-softmax gates for KV eviction; Qwen3-8B at 75% compression is 3.72 pts below full cache on RULER 8K

**What:** Each cached entry gets a learnable gate on its attention weight, placed inside the softmax so suppressing an entry redistributes its mass to the rest; a constant gate cancels out, so the frozen model is reproduced exactly. The authors show why naive training fails: under the LM loss the gate stays near a constant (the frozen backbone was fit to dense attention, so the loss favors the no-op), and once it moves the gradient also penalizes it for errors the frozen model already makes. Their fix keeps dense attention out of the gate's reach and trains against the dense model's predictions; under that setup, extra capacity (bigger scorer, access to the past, trainable backbone) does not help. Evicted content is partly recovered with parameter-free centroids read inside the same softmax, which works better than memory reads outside it.

**Result:** Qwen3-8B at 75% KV compression: 90.14 on RULER at 8K and 46.03 on LongBench, 3.72 and 1.36 points below full-cache inference.

**Critique:** The abstract compares only to full-cache inference, not to SnapKV, H2O or other learned eviction methods, so it is unclear whether this beats existing work. RULER is evaluated only at 8K, which is short for an eviction paper; behavior at 64K+ is not reported in the abstract. One model (Qwen3-8B). Code is linked (github.com/Chooovy/IndexMem).

### 3. [LowRAM: Low-Rank Keys and Amortised Values for Composable KV Cache Compression at Test-Time](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07025)

arXiv · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.07025
_Teresa Delgado, Hugo Hazard, Martin Benfeghoul, Jun Wang, Zafeirios Fountas_

**In one line:** LowRAM predicts values from keys per head at test time: 4x near-lossless value compression, up to 5x peak decode throughput

**What:** Observation: within an attention head, values are largely predictable from their keys. Value amortisation fits a lightweight per-head predictor at test time and stores residuals only for the tokens it reconstructs worst. The predictor can be meta-learned for initialization (lower prefill overhead) or fitted from scratch when a one-off cost is fine (e.g. many queries over one context). Combined with cross-layer low-rank key compression this gives LowRAM, an asymmetric K/V pipeline. Each decode step reconstructs only a selected subset of KV pairs to keep overhead low, and the method composes with quantization and eviction.

**Result:** 4x near-lossless value compression; up to 5x higher peak decode throughput at 4x compression; with quantization and eviction stacked, 26.9x compression at 94.5% of baseline performance on long contexts, across three model families.

**Critique:** The abstract names no baseline methods (KIVI, Palu, xKV etc.), so the 26.9x figure cannot be placed against the state of the art. 'Peak' throughput hides the prefill cost of fitting the predictor; the abstract leaves the overhead unquantified. Model sizes and the long-context benchmarks are not named on the abstract page. No code link.

### 4. [Accelerating Long-Context Decoding in Large-Scale Hybrid LLMs Without Retraining](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06133)

arXiv · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.06133
_João Eduardo Batista, Emil Vatai, Mohamed Wahib_

**In one line:** Training-free sparse attention for the full-attention layers of hybrid LLMs: ~2.1-2.2x decode speedup at 256K, 4B to 397B models

**What:** In hybrid linear/full-attention models the few remaining full-attention layers dominate decode time at long context. The paper replaces them, without training or weight changes, with a combined sparse pattern: a sliding window of recent tokens, a pinned prefix, and a fixed budget of the most relevant tokens. A multi-objective search picks window size and budget per layer.

**Result:** Across six hybrid models from three families (4B to 397B), accuracy matches each model's unmodified baseline on 21 long-context datasets. Decode speedups at 64K / 256K: Qwen3.5-4B up to 1.26x / 2.22x, Qwen3.5-122B-A10B 1.20x / 2.23x, Ornith-1.0-397B 1.16x / 2.06x.

**Critique:** The only comparison is the unmodified model; no head-to-head with other training-free sparse methods (Quest, DuoAttention-style window+sink, RetrievalAttention). Speedups at 64K are modest (1.16-1.26x). The per-layer search cost and how relevant tokens are selected (and its overhead) are not detailed in the abstract. No code link.

### 5. [Beyond K/V Caches: Cross-Model State Handoff in Hybrid Linear-Attention LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08133)

arXiv · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.08133
_Jiacheng Cai, Tao Zhang, Siqi Liang, Yun Dai, Ziqian Zeng_

**In one line:** Hybrid State Transfer hands recurrent, conv and K/V state from Qwen3.5 4B/9B to 27B, skipping re-prefill; 21.7x handoff speedup at 16K

**What:** Systems that switch models mid-conversation must re-prefill the whole context on the new model. Prior work transfers only full-attention K/V caches, but hybrid linear-attention models also keep recurrent states and short-convolution histories. HST maps all three: structured maps for recurrent states, order-preserving alignment for conv states, and adapters calibrated against the frozen target model's continuation outputs, with full-attention cache-map updates merged offline.

**Result:** Mean task retention over five benchmarks: 89.88% for Qwen3.5 4B→27B and 94.11% for 9B→27B. With source caches available, handoff speedup on synthetic 16K inputs is 21.66x and 21.91x.

**Critique:** Losing 6-10% of task performance is a lot for a serving optimization; a cheaper baseline (e.g. partial re-prefill of recent tokens) is not compared in the abstract. Only within-family transfer (Qwen3.5) is tested, and the speedup is measured on synthetic inputs and only for the handoff body. No code link.

### 6. [Give the Drafter a Second Chance to Guess: Parallel Reconsideration for Speculative Decoding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05724)

arXiv · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.05724
_Hoang Anh Duy Le, Huibing Dong, Alireza Khadem, Ziteng Sun, Xiang Song, Yizheng Huang et al._

**In one line:** Parallel Reconsideration lets feature-level drafters re-guess uncertain tokens in parallel passes: +5.3-10.2% acceptance length

**What:** Feature-level drafters (EAGLE-style) produce every draft token in one forward pass regardless of difficulty, and many rejected tokens are ones the drafter itself was unsure about. Parallel Reconsideration uses the drafter's own uncertainty to run extra passes on hesitant guesses alongside the one-pass draft, adding refined candidates to the draft tree. It comes with a training recipe and a serving-time drafting algorithm.

**Result:** Averaged across models and benchmarks: 5.3-10.2% longer acceptance length and 3.5-9.2% higher speedup than the baselines.

**Critique:** The abstract does not name the baselines, models or batch sizes, so the gains cannot be placed against EAGLE-3 or block drafters. Single-digit speedup gains at an unknown batch size may shrink under batched serving where extra drafter passes are not free. No code link.

### 8. [Who Asks, Who Answers: Linear Attention Builds the Query in Hybrid Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01909)

arXiv · paper · architecture · arXiv:2611.01909
_Wenqi Zeng, Chen Feng, YuAng Chen_

**In one line:** In Qwen3.5 and Granite4.0 hybrids, linear-attention blocks build the query that softmax layers use for in-context retrieval

**What:** Challenges the view that in hybrid models softmax attention does retrieval while linear layers just compress context. Studying Qwen3.5 (Gated DeltaNet) and Granite4.0 (Mamba-2), the authors find an 'Encode-Address' circuit: an early Encode block preserves token identity, and an Address block builds the final-position representation that a softmax 'reader' turns into its query. The Address block's recurrent state carries the retrieval target; at larger scales Address computation spreads over a chain of blocks, each guiding a successive reader.

**Result:** Transplanting the Address representation between prompts with different queried keys redirects retrieval in 0.97-0.99 of cases. Swapping Granite's Address block for Qwen's restores retrieval accuracy to 0.92-0.94 with no weight updates. Pattern holds across six Qwen3.5 sizes.

**Critique:** Mechanistic evidence comes from synthetic key-value retrieval prompts; whether the circuit explains retrieval on natural long-context tasks is not shown in the abstract. Only two model families. Useful for hybrid-architecture design (where to place softmax layers), but the abstract makes no design recommendation. Code is linked (github.com/yuang-chen/LinearQuery). Submitted 1 Nov; not covered earlier.

### 9. [Aligning Training and Rollout Policies for End-to-End FP4 RL on Mixture-of-Experts LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04376)

arXiv · paper · training · arXiv:2611.04376
_Hei Yi Mak, Hoang Le, Mehran Taghian Jazi, Yunke Peng, Huan Lin, Zhengzhi Tang et al._

**In one line:** FP4 RL on MoE: dense-model recovery recipes fail because routing breaks train/rollout alignment; MoeResQ applies symmetric fixes

**What:** Studies end-to-end FP4 RL post-training for MoE LLMs. A recovery recipe that works for dense models gives no gain over naive FP4 on MoE. The authors trace this to the router's discrete expert choices: train/inference policy alignment becomes very sensitive to whether corrections are applied symmetrically on both sides, and the dense recipe (correcting only the rollout side) degrades alignment sharply. They measure alignment by the Pearson correlation of train vs inference token probabilities and by router-selection match rate, and propose MoeResQ, which enforces symmetric corrections on training and rollout.

**Result:** The abstract reports no numbers: it says MoeResQ restores alignment and substantially narrows the accuracy gap to full precision, versus the naive FP4 baseline and the dense recipe.

**Critique:** No quantitative results, model sizes or tasks on the abstract page, so the size of the remaining gap to BF16 is unknown. Whether symmetric correction costs training throughput (the point of FP4) is not stated. No code link. Submitted 3 Nov; not covered earlier.

### 10. [A Scaling Theory of Multi-Epoch Pretraining: Late-Time Overfitting and Ensembling](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03590)

arXiv · paper · scaling · arXiv:2611.03590
_Zhen Yang, Yidi Miao, Blake Bordelon_

**In one line:** Multi-epoch scaling theory: logit-averaged ensembles delay late overfitting and beat wider or deeper single models at fixed data

**What:** Asks how epoch count and ensemble size jointly affect generalization when data is fixed. FineWeb LM experiments: averaging ensemble members' logits reduces late-training degradation and delays the optimal stopping point. Theory: a dynamical mean-field theory of multi-epoch SGD in power-law random-feature ensembles that tracks error correlations across epochs and members; ensembling cuts the prediction variance that drives late test-error growth. From the resulting stopping times the authors derive compute-allocation laws at fixed data and model size.

**Result:** At matched parameter-token budgets over a common horizon, ensembles outperform wider or deeper single models (abstract gives no numbers). Under the derived allocation, ensemble size should grow with compute at least as fast as per-member training time, and excess loss above the limit falls as a power of compute.

**Critique:** The abstract gives no model sizes or loss numbers for the FineWeb runs, so the practical margin over a single larger model is unknown. Ensembles multiply inference cost, which the matched-training-budget comparison ignores. Theory is in a random-feature model, a long way from transformers. Submitted 2 Nov; earlier runs found it but could not read it.

### 11. [MIT CSAIL 'Weight-Space Programming': GPT-6 Astra edits Qwen3.5-0.8B weights directly](https://runtimewire.com/article/gpt-6-astra-edits-qwen-weights-weight-space-programming)

RuntimeWire · news · other

**In one line:** GPT-6 Astra edited 131,072 Qwen3.5-0.8B weights without gradients; reported MATH-500 rose from 7.9% to 48.1%

**What:** Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang and Omar Khattab (MIT CSAIL) gave GPT-6 Astra Qwen3.5-0.8B's weights and a Python kernel (no GPU) and asked it to improve math. Astra traced the model's repetitive looping to one direction written by MLPs in layers 15 and 19 and subtracted it from 64 output columns per layer (0.016% of weights), using GSM8K/MATH training problems only. Other reported experiments: naming game policies from raw weights, fixing a stuck PPO car policy by editing 3.5% of weights, and Astra-written MinAtar networks beating long-trained PPO.

**Result:** Author-reported, 50 forward passes: MATH-500 7.9%→48.1%, GSM8K 13.8%→67.1%, IFEval 21.0%→62.3%; MATH-500 answers hitting the 32,768-token cap fell from 93% to 33.1%.

**Critique:** Rests on secondary coverage; the authors' blog post could not be fetched in this run. The gain mostly removes a looping failure rather than adding math skill: Astra failed to improve Gemma 3 270M, which rarely loops (about one point at most). Matthew Honnibal argues the 'raw weights' framing is misleading. No independent replication. Announced 2 Nov.
