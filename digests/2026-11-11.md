### 2. [Detent: Batch-Invariant LLM Serving and RL Rollouts at Near-Stock Speed](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10611)

arXiv · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.10611
_Peixu Xin, Runchuan Feng, Jiazhen Guo, Kan Wu, Ming Yin_

**In one line:** Detent: batch-invariant vLLM/SGLang serving at 1.04-1.17x stock per-token latency, vs 1.67-10.3x for built-in deterministic modes

**What:** Reproducible eval and on-policy RL need an engine whose output bits do not depend on which other requests share the batch. Batch-invariant kernels exist, but engines still pick kernels and parameters (split sizes, tiles) from batch contents, and different kernels round the same sum differently; engines' deterministic modes avoid this by forcing one conservative kernel per operator. Detent expresses kernel selection as a program the engine runs, finds the smallest set of batch-dependent values that must be pinned, and tunes those values per workload before deployment. The trainer can reuse the same kernels and values so rollout logprobs match.

**Result:** On Llama-3.1-70B and Qwen3-30B-A3B (multi-GPU, vLLM and SGLang), Detent is batch-invariant at 1.04-1.17x stock latency per output token, vs 1.67-10.3x for the engines' deterministic modes. Matches SGLang deterministic mode on GSM8K. Seeded rollouts up to Llama-3.1-70B replay bit for bit across batches and cache splits; a trainer using Detent's kernels matches every rollout logprob at 1.03x stock rollout time per token. In BF16 GRPO, Detent's run stays stable where stock collapses.

**Critique:** Read from the abstract only: GPU models, counts and TP degrees are not named there. The 1.04-1.17x figure is 'tuned per load', so latency under a load the tuning did not see is unclear. Accuracy parity is shown on GSM8K only. The GRPO collapse comparison appears to be a single run per setting; no code link on the abstract page.

### 3. [Coverage-Verified Sparse Attention: Closed-Loop Quality Control for Long-Context LLM Decoding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10634)

arXiv · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.10634
_Yan-Cheng Li, Chun-Yi Lee_

**In one line:** CVSA checks per-head softmax coverage during sparse decoding, fixes only low-coverage heads; 6.21x median decode-step speedup at 128K

**What:** Training-free sparse attention methods pick a token subset and never check whether it kept enough attention mass. CVSA defines coverage, the fraction of softmax mass on the selected tokens, and ties it to per-step output error. Coverage is used both as an accept/reject test per head and as the interpolation weight for recovery: high-coverage heads keep the sparse result, and only low-coverage heads are corrected. No sampling, draft model or speculative-decoding machinery is needed.

**Result:** On LongBench at 7B and 70B, within 1.13 points of dense attention, statistically tied at 70B; -0.8 points on RULER. Quality moves only 0.14 points across the threshold sweep. Median decode-step speedup 6.21x at 128K context.

**Critique:** Abstract-level read only. Speedup is a median per decode step, not end-to-end throughput or latency, and the comparison is against dense attention rather than named sparse baselines (e.g. Quest, vAttention, which also does verified sparse attention). Computing coverage needs some estimate of the full softmax normalizer; how that is obtained cheaply, and its overhead, is not visible from the abstract. Base model families are not named; no code link.

### 4. [SGA-Sparse: Training Native SwiGLU Gates for Activation-Sparse and Low-Bit LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11223)

arXiv (cs.LG listing) · paper · efficiency · arXiv:2611.11223
_Ruokai Yin, Steven Li_

**In one line:** SGA-Sparse trains SwiGLU gate projections as top-k routers: 75-85% FFN channel sparsity on 1B-8B LLaMA within 0.2-2.7 pp of dense

**What:** Converts pretrained SwiGLU LLMs into fixed-budget activation-sparse models by training the existing gate projection as a top-k router over FFN channels, so each token activates a fixed number of channels. It is combined with quantization-aware training in two recipes: continued QAT for 4-bit weights, and joint QAT for sub-4-bit targets, giving sparse+low-bit models with 2.5- and 1.5-bit-equivalent cost.

**Result:** After about 30B training tokens, LLaMA models from 1B to 8B reach 75-85% per-token FFN channel sparsity at 0.2-2.7 pp below dense checkpoints on average zero-shot accuracy. The 2.5- and 1.5-bit-equivalent models match dense 3-bit QAT and approach dense 2-bit accuracy; 1.5-bit-equivalent beats dense ternary models by 1.3 pp on average.

**Critique:** Based on the arXiv listing abstract only; the abstract page itself was rate-limited. 'Bit-equivalent' folds sparsity into a bit budget, so it is not the same as storage or latency; no wall-clock speedups or kernels are reported in the abstract. Evaluation is average zero-shot accuracy, which hides task-level losses (the 2.7 pp end of the range is sizeable), and nothing beyond 8B or on long-context or reasoning tasks is shown.

### 5. [The Surprising Effectiveness of Deleting Weights in LLM Post-Training](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10866)

arXiv · paper · training · arXiv:2611.10866
_Jack Lu, Zhenbang Yang, Mike Lasby, Tejas Pote, Yani Ioannou, Mengye Ren_

**In one line:** Deletion Tuning: zeroing under 0.05% of LLM weights via a learned gate-projection mask matches full fine-tuning in RLVR, SFT and OPD

**What:** Asks how little of a pretrained LLM must change to gain a capability. Deletion Tuning learns only a binary mask over the gate projection in each transformer block and zeroes the masked weights, with no other updates. It is tested in three post-training regimes: RL with verifiable rewards, supervised fine-tuning and on-policy distillation, and the authors compare the geometry of learned weight changes (stable rank, spectrum drift, principal-weight overlap) with FFT, LoRA and random sparse fine-tuning.

**Result:** Masking fewer than 0.05% of weights matches full fine-tuning 'at scale' in all three regimes; the mask artifact is hundreds to thousands of times smaller than an FFT checkpoint. Under RL, prior-task accuracy stays near the base model, while FFT, LoRA and random sparse fine-tuning lose up to 5.4 points. On the geometry measures, Deletion Tuning is closest to FFT.

**Critique:** The abstract names no base models, sizes, datasets or task scores, so 'matches FFT at scale' cannot be checked from it. Training a binary mask still needs gradients through a dense parameterization (memory and compute cost of mask training are not given). Only the gate projection is masked; whether the result depends on SwiGLU-style models is untested in the abstract. No code link on the abstract page.

### 6. [What the Loss Curve Misses: FP4 Pretraining Slows the Acquisition of Rare Facts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12832)

arXiv · paper · training · arXiv:2611.12832
_Chang Lu, Mengjiao Zhang_

**In one line:** NVFP4 pretraining needs 1.32-1.52x the exposures of BF16 to learn rare facts while validation loss differs by only 0.5-2%

**What:** Low-precision pretraining is usually judged by validation loss. The authors train transformers from scratch with emulated 4-bit matmuls on synthetic biographies with controlled exposure counts (alone and mixed into web text) and measure how many exposures each format needs to reach a given fact-recall level. Noise-injection controls separate effects of backward-pass quantization, noise in Adam's second moment, and noise in the update itself.

**Result:** NVFP4 needs 1.32x and 1.52x the exposures of BF16 to match recall in the two settings, while validation loss differs by 0.5% and 2%. Evaluating both with BF16 matmuls lowers this to 1.26x and 1.30x; at matched loss the primary readout gives about 1.2x. Update-noise carries about two-thirds of the cost; raising the learning rate does not recover it. Gradient statistics from BF16 checkpoints rank formats and noise controls with Spearman 0.96, without fitting.

**Critique:** Model size and token counts are not in the abstract, and FP4 is emulated, not run on FP4 hardware, so kernel-specific effects (scaling, rounding modes) may differ. Synthetic biographies are a narrow proxy for real long-tail knowledge. Only NVFP4 is reported among 4-bit recipes in the abstract; mitigations used in practice (stochastic rounding, Hadamard transforms, BF16 final phase) are not shown to be tested.

### 7. [Translating KV Caches Between Hybrid-Attention Language Models: MLA Latents, Linear-Attention States and Sparse-Attention Indexers](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.12062)

arXiv · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.12062
_Povilas Kvedaras, Tomas Rasymas_

**In one line:** Learned translators map MLA latents, Gated DeltaNet states and sparse-indexer keys between models; 32.8x faster TTFT than re-prefill at 32K

**What:** When a conversation is handed to another model, the target normally re-reads the whole history. Prior KV-cache translation only handled GQA caches. This work translates the cache formats of newer open models between two frozen models that share a tokenizer: token-wise linear maps on un-rotated MLA latents (Kimi-VL-A3B <-> Kimi-Linear-48B-A3B), token ids decoded from the source's layer-0 latent to supply exact token identity, linear-attention states copied with a closed-form bilinear correction when geometries match (Qwen3.5-27B <-> Qwen3.8-27B, Gated DeltaNet hybrids) or rebuilt by replaying the target recurrence, and predicted sparse-attention indexer keys (Qwen3.8-27B -> Qwen3.8-Flash-Next). Maps are fit in closed form, then fine-tuned with KL self-distillation against the target reading its own cache.

**Result:** Adding token identity raises needle recall from 0.09 to 0.99 at 2-32K (Pair A) and 0.83-1.00 at 64K, 8x the longest training prompt. Hybrid->full-MLA is within the confidence interval of native quality on every task metric; same-geometry GDN hybrids reach native level both ways. TTFT is 18.7x and 46.8x shorter than re-prefill at 64K; 208 ms vs 6,823 ms (32.8x) on Pair B at 32K. Predicted indexer keys: recall 0.87/0.84 vs 0.88/0.85 with native keys.

**Critique:** Several headline results rest on one seed, and Pair C (sparse indexer) is smaller-scale and one direction only. Requires a shared tokenizer and closely related model families; extension to 300B+ models is stated as a hypothesis. GDN hybrids inherit 6% refusals from the source. Overlaps with the cross-model state handoff paper covered on 8 Nov (2611.08133); a head-to-head comparison is not visible. Code release not confirmed.

### 8. [MACaQ: Fast, Model Agnostic Calibrated Quantization for Low-Precision Weights and Activations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10649)

arXiv (cs.LG listing) · paper · efficiency · arXiv:2611.10649

**In one line:** MACaQ: per-layer MXFP4 W4A4 PTQ with block-Hadamard and FP4 code flipping; quantizes a 122B model in 64 min on 32.5 GB GPU memory

**What:** Post-training MXFP4 W4A4 quantization that treats each linear layer independently, so it needs no architecture-specific handling. Activations get a per-channel scale and an online block-Hadamard transform whose inverses are folded into the weights. 'Flip-Refinement' is a Hessian-aware coordinate-descent step that moves individual FP4 codes to adjacent representable values to reduce layer output error.

**Result:** Lower perplexity than DuQuant++ and MR-GPTQ; on Llama-3-8B matches their best perplexity with 11x less quantization time. Adds 2.3% end-to-end overhead over pure MXFP4 on Qwen3-32B in vLLM. Quantizes a 122B-parameter model in 64 minutes with 32.5 GB of GPU RAM; also applied unchanged to an image diffusion model.

**Critique:** Based on the arXiv listing abstract only; abstract and HTML pages were not reachable this run. Results are given as perplexity, with no downstream or reasoning accuracy in the abstract, and the size of the gap to BF16 is not stated. The 2.3% overhead is relative to pure MXFP4, not to BF16, and the GPU is not named. Authors not recorded here.

### 9. [CALFA-KV: Joint Calibration and Fisher-Guided Anchors for 1-Bit KV Cache Compression](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10511)

arXiv (cs.LG listing) · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.10511
_Xiancai Tian, Leichao Zhang, Shenhui Zhang, Yi Shan, Hailong Li, Feng Lin et al._

**In one line:** CALFA-KV: calibrated 1-bit KV codebooks plus Fisher-scored FP16 anchors; 1.25 bits/channel within 0.90-2.01 points of BF16

**What:** Offline, CALFA-KV jointly fits a key center, radial normalization parameters and K/V codebooks so the quantized model's projected attention output matches the frozen model's, and estimates channel-wise Fisher sensitivities from squared gradients of a next-token loss. Online, these sensitivities weight each KV state's reconstruction error, and the highest-scoring states (one token's K/V in one layer and head) are kept in FP16 under a per-layer, per-KV-head budget. The authors bound the excess risk of score-based anchor selection versus the optimum at the same budget.

**Result:** With 56 extra FP16 anchors per layer and KV head, CALFA-KV (d8b8) beats all evaluated nominal 1-bit baselines on mean score over seven benchmarks for each of three models. CALFA-KV (d8b10), at a nominal 1.25 bits per channel, narrows the gap to BF16 to 0.90-2.01 points.

**Critique:** Based on the arXiv listing abstract only; the abstract page was rate-limited twice. Models, context lengths and baseline names are not given there. 'Nominal' bit rates exclude the FP16 anchors and codebook metadata, so the real memory footprint is higher. No latency or throughput numbers, and anchor scoring at decode time adds work whose cost is not reported.

### 10. [D3Tree: Learning to Prune Block-Diffusion Draft Trees for Efficient Verification](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11371)

arXiv (cs.LG listing) · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.11371
_Hong Nie, Zhangchi Gao, Jun Yu, Bo Wang, Jianqing Sun, Shengping Liu_

**In one line:** D3Tree prunes block-diffusion speculative draft trees with a 2H+2-parameter head; 1.3-1.8x over autoregressive at 64 concurrent requests

**What:** Block-diffusion drafters propose large token trees, but verifying every node wastes compute when the server is busy. D3Tree adds a tiny tree-size head (2H+2 parameters) that predicts each candidate node's conditional survival probability, and under heavy load allocates a batch-wide verification budget by predicted survival instead of a fixed per-request tree.

**Result:** Across six vision-language verifiers and 17 text and multimodal benchmarks, fastest in 70 of 72 load settings. 3.2-4.0x speedup over autoregressive decoding for one request and 1.3-1.8x at 64 concurrent requests, where fixed-budget trees drop below autoregressive speed. Lowest mean end-to-end latency in every phase of a time-varying arrival trace.

**Critique:** Based on the arXiv listing abstract only. Verifiers are vision-language models; results on large text-only LLMs and on standard drafters (EAGLE-style) are not shown in the abstract. Baselines for 'fastest' are not named, and hardware and serving engine are not stated. Overhead of training the head and the drafter is not reported.
