### 1. [KVPath: Connecting Key-Value Representations Across Transformer Layers](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2611.16072)

arXiv · paper · architecture · arXiv:2611.16072
_Xuan Luo, Zhiyuan Wang, Hanzhong Cao, Xifeng Yan_

**In one line:** KVPath stores one full KV at layer 1 and small delta KVs above it, cutting KV cache up to 68% (GQA) and 55% (MLA) at matched params

**What:** Observes that a token's keys and values are strongly correlated across neighbouring layers, so later layers can be expressed as small updates. KVPath changes the attention block so that only the first layer keeps a full-dimensional base KV; each later layer stores a low-dimensional delta KV that is combined with the running representation. Two variants: KVPath-Residual (values updated by residual addition) and KVPath-Rolling (feature dimensions updated progressively). This is an architectural change trained in, not a post-hoc compression.

**Result:** At matched parameter counts, KV-cache storage drops by up to 68% relative to Grouped-Query Attention and up to 55% relative to Multi-Head Latent Attention, with quality reported as maintained or improved. The listing abstract gives no model sizes, token budgets or benchmark scores.

**Critique:** Read from the listing abstract only; the abstract page fetch was declined. 'Up to' figures with no scale, data budget or task list stated, so it is unclear whether gains hold at billion-parameter scale or on long-context retrieval, where cross-layer sharing schemes often degrade. No comparison named against other cross-layer KV-sharing methods (e.g. CLA, YOCO-style designs). Decode latency and kernel cost of reconstructing per-layer KVs from deltas are not mentioned.

### 2. [Symmetry in Time: A Representation Theory of Linear Recurrent Memory](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2611.17162)

arXiv · paper · architecture · arXiv:2611.17162
_Anif N. Shikder, Ramit Dey, Jan Minac, Lyle E. Muller_

**In one line:** Diagonal SSMs can only track commutative groups; HyperSSM adds quaternion channels and solves S5 tracking where prior SSMs are at chance

**What:** Uses representation theory to explain why diagonal state-space models (real or complex per-channel recurrences, as in Mamba-style scans) fail at state tracking: their transitions commute, so the state records which inputs occurred but not their order. The paper proves an SSM tracks a group at all lengths exactly when the group is a quotient of the group generated by its transition matrices, and that tracking a non-commutative group needs transitions that split into real, complex and quaternion blocks. HyperSSM adds quaternion channels to the recurrence while keeping linear-time inference and parallel training.

**Result:** Trained only on final answers, HyperSSM solves the group-tracking tasks of recent state-tracking benchmarks exactly with at most five state numbers, including S5 with 32 generators where previous diagonal SSMs are at chance, and generalises to sequences 25x longer than seen in training. The learned weights are also shown to be readable (gyroscope calibration, imperfect qubit gate rotations, protein backbone geometry).

**Critique:** Synthetic group-tracking tasks only; no language-modelling perplexity or downstream LLM results, so it is unknown whether quaternion channels help or cost anything at scale. Throughput of the quaternion scan versus diagonal scans is not reported. Baselines are described only as previous diagonal SSMs; comparison to other non-diagonal fixes (e.g. negative-eigenvalue or Householder-product recurrences such as DeltaNet variants) is not stated in the abstract. Read from the listing abstract.

### 3. [GC-MoE on SM120: Overlapping Expert-Parallel Token Dispatch with Warp-Level FP8xFP4 Block-Scaled MMA on Consumer Blackwell](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2611.16219)

arXiv · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.16219
_Yong Li, Haoyu Wang, Kan Liu, Tao Lan_

**In one line:** Fused expert-parallel MoE decode kernel for consumer Blackwell (SM120): 1.52x over FlashInfer W4A8 MegaMoE on 8x RTX PRO 5000 over PCIe

**What:** Rebuilds a fused expert-parallel MoE decode operator for SM120 GPUs, which expose only warp-level block-scaled tensor-core MMA (not the datacenter Blackwell instructions most MoE kernels target) and connect over PCIe rather than NVLink. Token dispatch across GPUs is overlapped with the grouped expert GEMM inside one kernel, using FP4 weights and FP8 activations with block scaling.

**Result:** On 8x RTX PRO 5000 over PCIe, 1.522x faster than FlashInfer CuTe DSL W4A8 MegaMoE and 1.387x faster than Cake MegaMoE across 18 decode points, winning all 18 against each; 1.388x faster than Cake with shared experts fused. Numerical checks reported as passing.

**Critique:** Operator-level decode benchmarks on one hardware configuration; no end-to-end tokens/s or model-quality numbers for a full MoE model are given in the abstract. Which MoE shapes (expert count, hidden size, top-k) the 18 points cover is not stated in the listing. FP4 weights imply a quantised model, so accuracy impact rests on the chosen quantisation recipe, not evaluated here. Abstract page fetch was declined; details from the listing.

### 4. [Fewer Expert Transfers Without Changing Routing: Expert Eviction for MoE Offloading](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2611.15842)

arXiv · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.15842
_Pierre-Luc Bacon, Diego Calanzone, Jean-Maxime Larouche_

**In one line:** MoE offloading: evicting the expert used farthest in the future copies 2.1x fewer experts than LRU on OLMoE-1B-7B, routing unchanged

**What:** In MoE offloading, experts live in CPU memory and are copied to the GPU on demand. Prior work retrains or biases routers to reuse experts and treats fewer routing switches as a proxy for fewer copies. The authors measure actual CPU-to-GPU copies and instead leave routing untouched, changing only the GPU expert-cache eviction rule to a Belady-style policy: evict the expert whose next use is farthest away, using a short lookahead of upcoming expert demand.

**Result:** On OLMoE-1B-7B with real transfers on an L40S, every tested routing-altering policy that decodes faster than LRU raised next-token loss by at least 0.067 nats. The eviction rule copies 2.1x fewer experts than LRU with no quality change and is within 3.5% of the minimum possible copies. A 16-token lookahead recovers 96% of the reduction, versus 27% for a rule based on past request counts.

**Critique:** One small model (OLMoE-1B-7B) and one GPU. The rule needs future expert demand; how that lookahead is obtained in real autoregressive decoding (where future routing depends on tokens not yet generated) needs checking in the paper. Reports copies, not end-to-end latency or tokens/s versus systems like MoE-Infinity or Fiddler. Workshop paper (NeurIPS 2026 AXIOM workshop); code is released.

### 5. [Read Once, Answer Many: Parallel Prompting for Shared-Context LLM Inference](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2611.15130)

arXiv · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.15130
_Zekun Zhao, Jeffrey Flanigan_

**In one line:** Parallel Prompting decodes many questions over one document in a single request, reading its KV once per step: 3.7x over SGLang on A100

**What:** For many independent questions over one long document, prefix caching removes duplicate prefill but each decode step still reads the document's KV cache once per question, making decode bandwidth-bound and linear in question count. Parallel Prompting decodes P questions as P streams inside one request; a stream-id attention mask evaluated inside the attention kernel keeps each answer conditioned only on the document and its own question, so the shared KV is read once per step for all streams. Implemented in SGLang in about 80 lines plus a small engine package, with fixed-shape decode steps captured as CUDA graphs.

**Result:** CodeLlama-7B, 8k-16k-token documents, 64-256 questions: 3.7x faster than stock SGLang with the FlashInfer backend on an A100 and 5.9-6.0x on an RTX A6000, faster in all 144 timed runs (2 GPUs x 2 SGLang releases x 6 workloads x 6 input sets).

**Critique:** Single older model (CodeLlama-7B, MHA-style KV); gains may shrink on GQA/MLA models whose KV is already small. Baseline is stock SGLang rather than existing shared-prefix attention kernels (cascade/shared-prefix attention in FlashInfer, Hydragen-style batching), which target the same bandwidth waste. Answer-equivalence to independent decoding is implied by the mask but output-quality checks are not described in the abstract.

### 6. [SlipStream: Enabling HBF for GPU Systems by Mitigating the Latency and SRAM Overheads](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2611.14776)

arXiv (cross-list from cs.AR) · paper · inference · arXiv:2611.14776
_Anish Saxena, Hritvik Taneja, Christos Kozyrakis, Aamer Jaleel_

**In one line:** SlipStream prefetcher hides High-Bandwidth Flash latency for LLM inference: 92.4% of ideal with a 40 MB buffer vs 43.1% for H3 at 320 MB

**What:** High-Bandwidth Flash (HBF) replaces HBM's DRAM dies with NAND for up to 16x capacity at similar read bandwidth, but ~20 us read latency can slow inference up to 12.8x. SlipStream is a software-hardware co-designed prefetcher: software profiles the repeatable weight-page demand order of data-independent LLM kernels and inserts a staging warp that replays it with non-blocking STAGE instructions; hardware treats the staging warp as producer and work warps as consumers, retiring entries when demand first touches a page and tracking 4 KB pages instead of cache lines.

**Result:** Across seven dense models from 16 to 800 GB, a 40 MB staging buffer reaches 92.4% of an ideal HBF with HBM-like latency, versus 43.1% for the H3 tensor-streaming design even with 320 MB. A 16 MB buffer is within 4% of 40 MB; MoE models reach 91.2% of ideal.

**Critique:** Simulation study of hardware (HBF and the STAGE instruction) that does not exist in shipping GPUs; results depend on the simulator's HBF model. Relies on data-independent weight access order, which MoE routing partly breaks, and the MoE result's assumptions are not detailed in the abstract. Accepted to HPCA 2027.

### 7. [Factored One-sided Adam-Moment for Practical and Scalable SOAP](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2611.16905)

arXiv / HF Daily Papers · paper · training · arXiv:2611.16905
_Jaemyung Yu, Byeongho Heo, Sangdoo Yun, Dongyoon Han_

**In one line:** FOAM: one-sided, factored SOAP optimizer matches SOAP and Muon loss from 124M-760M with half SOAP's state and Muon-class wall-clock

**What:** SOAP (Adam in Shampoo's eigenbasis) converges fast per step but needs large optimizer state and two eigendecompositions per layer. FOAM makes two changes: input-only preconditioning (drops the output-side Kronecker factor, removing one eigendecomposition; exact when output-side curvature is diagonal) and a factored Adam second moment built from row and column statistics instead of SOAP's dense rotated moment. Under the K-FAC Fisher assumption with diagonal output curvature the factored estimator exactly recovers SOAP's moment.

**Result:** Matches SOAP and Muon validation loss in LLM pretraining from 124M to 760M parameters, with half of SOAP's optimizer state and Muon-class wall-clock time, robust over a 3x learning-rate range. Measured K-FAC violation is 15-20% with negligible effect on updates. No exact loss or speed figures in the abstract.

**Critique:** Largest model is 760M; optimizer rankings often change at multi-billion scale and long token horizons. The abstract gives no AdamW comparison numbers and no absolute loss deltas, so 'matches' cannot be sized. Hardware and token budgets are not stated in the listing. NeurIPS 2026; code released by NAVER AI.

### 8. [The Noise Floor of a Quantization Comparison: Engine, restart, batch and kernel drift, measured against 8-bit and 4-bit effects in two decision models](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2611.15671)

arXiv · paper · efficiency · arXiv:2611.15671
_Aleks Blagojevich, Trevor Sunderland_

**In one line:** Pre-registered study: engine, restart, batch and kernel drift flip up to 0.92% of decisions; 4-bit quant effects exceed that noise

**What:** Quantised models are usually compared with their BF16 originals while also changing engine, server instance, batching and sometimes the selected kernel. The authors measure those non-quantisation sources on 2,400 public decisions from two Cloudflare decision models, Clef-flash (9B) and Clef (27B), on an NVIDIA GB10, and report each quantisation effect as excess over this noise floor. Five predictions were pre-registered.

**Result:** Non-quantisation sources move 0-0.92% of decisions (llama.cpp restarts are bit-identical; vLLM restarts flip up to 20-22 NVFP4 decisions; an engine change flips 6-16, batch size 8-15, an auto-selected Gated DeltaNet kernel 10-14). Within one engine, decisions moved (Clef-flash / Clef): Q8_0 0.88% / 1.04%, FP8 2.12% / 1.62%, Q4_K_M 5.79% / 3.50%, NVFP4 5.79% / 5.67%; every 4-bit interval lies outside all noise sources. Clef's Q4_K_M changes accuracy by a net -0.02 points over 31,275 questions while 436 decisions get worse; NVFP4 costs 0.95 and 0.65 points.

**Critique:** Two models from one family on a single GPU type (GB10), and classification-style decisions rather than open-ended generation. Two of five pre-registered predictions failed on both models (FP8 is not within restart noise; an engine change moves fewer decisions than 8-bit quantisation). The 436-worse figure is labelled exploratory. Useful methodology; generality to other model families is untested. Code, recipes and per-record outputs are released.

### 9. [LazyWeights: Decode-on-Demand Edge LLMs](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2611.17108)

arXiv · paper · efficiency · arXiv:2611.17108
_Arnab Sanyal, Gourav Datta, Sandeep P. Chinchali_

**In one line:** LazyWeights Huffman-codes GPTQ/llama.cpp integer weights and decodes them on demand: 5.7-31.4% smaller than the best llama.cpp format

**What:** Deployed formats (GPTQ, AWQ, llama.cpp K/I-quants) spend equal bits on every integer code although code frequencies are far from uniform. LazyWeights keeps those formats, tunes GPTQ so the codes compress better, Huffman-codes them losslessly in interleaved lanes, and decodes on demand in a small unit between DRAM and L2, so GPU kernels are unchanged and DRAM moves only coded bytes.

**Result:** At 1% and 3.5% perplexity budgets, coded Phi-3-mini, Mistral-7B and SmolLM-1.7B are 5.7-31.4% smaller than the best llama.cpp format. A bit-exact 0.21 mm2 7 nm ASIC decoder (simulated) cuts Jetson Orin latency and energy per token by up to 36% and 38% (54% / 44% with prompt-lookup speculation). On a Jetson Nano, 58-64% less time and 53-57% less energy per token than CPU llama.cpp; a streamed Mistral-7B runs 6.6x faster at 3.89 bits per weight.

**Critique:** The largest speedups depend on a proposed hardware decoder unit that is not in shipping Jetsons (validated on FPGA, area/energy from 7 nm estimates). The Jetson Nano comparison is against CPU llama.cpp, a weak baseline for a GPU-equipped board. Short paper (6 pages), small models only, perplexity-only quality measure.

### 10. [Effective Teacher Support for Reasoning Distillation](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2611.14850)

arXiv (cross-list) · paper · training · arXiv:2611.14850
_Lavinia Ghita, Dhruv Desai_

**In one line:** Distilling from only the teacher's top 0.01% of vocabulary showed no MMLU-Pro drop vs full-vocab KD for Qwen3 4B to 1.7B

**What:** Forward-KL distillation computes and stores dense teacher and student distributions over the whole vocabulary at every position. The authors keep only the teacher's highest-probability fraction of the vocabulary (renormalising the retained mass) and sweep retained support from 100% down to 0.01%. Main study Qwen3 4B to 1.7B; confirmation on Qwen3 8B to 4B, Nemotron Nano 12B to 9B, and answer-only supervision.

**Result:** Qwen3: 0.01% support showed no observed MMLU-Pro degradation versus full-vocabulary distillation, though disagreement with the full teacher distribution rose. Nemotron Nano matched full-vocabulary MMLU-Pro at 0.1%; smaller fractions failed to emit the closing reasoning tag. At 0.01%, caching retained teacher scores cut training step time by 38% and restricting student output to those tokens cut peak memory by 24%.

**Critique:** 'No observed degradation' is judged mainly on MMLU-Pro (GPQA-Diamond secondary), a narrow view of reasoning quality; the rise in distribution disagreement suggests effects that benchmark accuracy may hide. The safe cutoff is model-family dependent (Nemotron breaks below 0.1%). Similar top-k logit distillation is already common practice; comparison to top-k with a fixed k rather than a vocabulary fraction is not described.

Together AI blog · news · inference

**Critique:** All numbers are Together's own and baselines are 'best open-source configurations' chosen by the vendor; no independent reproduction. Hardware per model is not specified on the blog, and compilation cost is not stated there (secondary coverage says runs can take 24 hours at twice a replica's resources). Proprietary service; no public release. The arXiv preprint itself could not be read (fetch declined), so the preprint date rests on secondary coverage.
