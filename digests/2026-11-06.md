### 1. [ERNIE-5.5-Flash-Base](https://huggingface.co/baidu/ERNIE-5.5-Flash-Base)

Hugging Face model card (Baidu) · release · architecture

**In one line:** Baidu ERNIE-5.5-Flash-Base weights: 280B-total/14B-active MoE, 37 compressed-attention + 6 MLA-DSA layers, 33.5T tokens

**What:** Base checkpoint (no post-training) released as open weights in FP8, with a separate BF16 repo. Per the model card: 43 layers (1 dense FFN + 42 MoE), hidden size 4096, 64 heads. Attention is hybrid: 37 'Heavily Compressed Attention' layers and 6 MLA layers with DeepSeek-style sparse attention (DSA). Each MoE layer has 512 routed experts with top-10 selection plus 1 shared expert, computed in a 2048-d latent space with multiple sigmoid routers ('Multi-Router Latent MoE'). There is no separate MTP module; 'ECHO' reuses the final layer to predict future tokens and doubles as a built-in speculative-decoding draft (0.36B extra parameters). Pretrained on 33.5T tokens at 128K context in FP8 mixed precision; the card claims 1M-token context for fine-tuning and inference. Release date (5 Nov) comes from The Decoder's coverage.

**Result:** Base-model scores vs DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Base / Hy3-preview-Base / MiMo-V2.5-Base: MMLU-Pro 78.4 vs 69.2 / 66.9 / 65.7; GPQA 60.2 vs 55.9 / 50.2 / 51.4; HumanEval+ 82.3 vs 69.5 / 78.0 / 74.4. It trails on ChineseSimpleQA (73.0 vs DeepSeek's 75.3) and BBH-CoT (89.2 vs Hy3-preview's 90.0).

**Critique:** All numbers are Baidu's own few-shot base-model evaluations; none are independently reproduced yet. The card gives no technical report, ablations or training-compute figures for the new parts (HCA, latent MoE, ECHO), and no measured draft acceptance rate or end-to-end speedup for ECHO. The 1M-token claim is beyond the 128K training context, and the card shows no long-context results to back it. The weights use a custom licence, not an OSI-approved one; secondary coverage (OrcaRouter) reports it excludes the EU, UK and South Korea and requires separate permission above US$50M related revenue, which was not checked against the licence text.

### 2. [CISA: Retrofitting Dense Attention Models with Chunk-Indexed Sparse Attention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03200)

arXiv · paper · inference

**In one line:** CISA retrofits dense-attention LLMs with chunk-level sparse attention, no gradient updates; 2.29x serving throughput at 1M tokens

**What:** Long-context decoding is bound by re-reading a growing KV cache. CISA makes pretrained dense-attention models sparse without any training: it scores fixed-size KV chunks with compact summaries of the existing keys rather than indexing individual tokens, so no separate full-length index cache is needed. One indexing query per KV group selects chunks shared by every query in that group. During prefill, 'QShare' reuses chunk selections across neighbouring queries to cut indexing cost and improve KV reuse and GPU utilization. Evaluated on NVIDIA Nemotron 3 Nano and Ultra and on Inkling-Small.

**Result:** Kernel level (one layer, BF16, batch 32, 1M-token prompt): core attention 11.7x faster for late prefill queries and 13.0x for decode; 8.6x and 3.0x once chunk scoring and selection are counted. Nemotron 3 Ultra in NVFP4 with a 4,096-chunk budget stays within 1.5 pp of dense on MRCR, LongBench v2 and GraphWalks. RULER up to 1M stays within 2.05 pp with 8-16x fewer KV reads. End to end on 4x B300 (1M-token prompts, 32 concurrent requests): 2.29x dense throughput, or 2.61x with a smaller budget, at the cost of the GraphWalks gap growing from 1.49 to 3.59 pp.

**Critique:** The only baseline is dense attention. There is no comparison with other training-free sparse methods (e.g. Quest-style page selection, MInference) or with trained sparse attention like DSA/NSA, so the gain from chunk indexing itself is unclear. The authors are NVIDIA, testing mostly on NVIDIA models and B300 hardware. The decode speedup drops from 13.0x to 3.0x once selection overhead counts. Quality is reported as aggregate gaps on a few long-context suites, with no short-context or reasoning regressions shown. No code is linked. Rests on the abstract page.

### 3. [LeanSparse: Memory-Efficient Serving for Native Sparse-Attention LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04671)

arXiv · paper · inference

**In one line:** LeanSparse offloads indexer keys and streams prefill history for DeepSeek-V4-Flash serving; 2.95x concurrency vs HiSparse at 950K

**What:** Natively sparse-attention models still need a lot of GPU memory for long-context serving. Existing KV-offloading systems keep the full history of indexer keys on the GPU, which caps decode concurrency, and prefill's temporary memory can block admission. LeanSparse (built in SGLang) streams each layer's history through two reusable GPU buffers during prefill, loading the next layer while the current one computes. For decode it uses coarse-to-fine indexing: it searches compact representations and fetches the original indexer keys only to refine candidates. Separate indexer-key and KV caches reuse data across steps.

**Result:** On DeepSeek-V4-Flash at 950K context: 2.95x the concurrent requests and 2.14x the decode throughput of HiSparse. On multi-turn Open-SWE traces, median TTFT drops from 21.6 s to 0.6 s and median session completion time falls 17.7% vs ECHO.

**Critique:** Evaluated on a single model (DeepSeek-V4-Flash), so it is unclear how far it carries to other sparse-attention designs. The abstract does not say whether coarse-to-fine indexing changes which tokens are selected, or report any accuracy check against exact indexing. The hardware and GPU count are not stated in the abstract. No code is linked. Rests on the abstract page.

### 4. [LoopVQ: Sub 1-bit KV Cache Quantization for Looped Transformers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05304)

arXiv · paper · efficiency

**In one line:** LoopVQ vector-quantizes looped-transformer KV caches jointly across loop steps at 0.5 bits/element; up to 6.1x SGLang throughput

**What:** Looped transformers store separate keys and values for each loop step, so the KV cache grows with the loop count and dominates memory. The authors find the cache highly redundant across loop steps, with steps differing in how sensitive they are to quantization. LoopVQ vector-quantizes channels from several loop steps jointly in one code, and fine-tunes the codebooks so outputs match full precision. It can be combined with TaSQ for even lower bit-widths. Tested on Ouro and Huginn looped models.

**Result:** At 0.5 bits per element it beats existing KV-cache vector-quantization methods on general, long chain-of-thought and long-context retrieval tasks. With TaSQ it keeps much of BF16 accuracy at 0.25 bits per element. In SGLang it nearly halves decode latency at 32K context and raises peak serving throughput up to 4.3x (Ouro 1.4B) and 6.1x (Ouro 2.6B) over BF16.

**Critique:** The abstract does not name the VQ baselines or give the accuracy numbers, so 'outperforms' and 'retains much of BF16 accuracy' cannot be quantified from it. Models are small (1.4B-2.6B Ouro, plus Huginn). The throughput gains are against an uncompressed BF16 cache rather than against 2-4-bit KV quantization or the LoopKV-style cross-loop reuse covered here on 5 Nov. Codebook fine-tuning adds a calibration step whose cost is not stated. No code link.

### 5. [Hinted Scaling Laws](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02270)

arXiv · paper · scaling

**In one line:** Hinted scaling laws add privileged hints so small models score above zero; GSM8K at 1e23 FLOPs predicted within 3 pp from ≤1e20 runs

**What:** Downstream-accuracy scaling laws break when small models score near zero, leaving no trend to extrapolate. The authors (Stanford, incl. Liang and Hashimoto) use privileged information, such as partial solutions, to turn a benchmark into a ladder of easier hinted versions. Accuracy rises smoothly with the hint level, so a joint fit over model scale and hint level can be extrapolated to the unhinted task at large scale.

**Result:** On the Delphi pretraining ladder, a hinted law fit on models of at most 1e20 FLOPs predicts the 1e23-FLOP model's GSM8K accuracy (25.9%) within 3 pp. A standard law on the same models under-forecasts by 13 pp, and is still 12 pp off with runs up to 1e22 in the fit. Across pretrained and post-trained families on GSM8K, AIME and HLE, median forecast error falls from 21 pp to 4.3 pp. One hinted rollout ranks near-identical models about as well as five unhinted ones.

**Critique:** The headline extrapolation is a single target point on one benchmark and one model ladder. Hints need privileged solution data, which limits it to tasks with reference solutions. The abstract does not name the post-trained families, or show whether the hint construction transfers across tasks without per-benchmark tuning. No code is linked. Submitted 1 Nov and not covered by earlier runs.

### 6. [What We Keep of What We Forget: Replacement Operators and Routing Semantics in Training-Free MoE Expert Pruning](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.41366)

HF Daily Papers · paper · efficiency

**In one line:** MoE pruning study: what replaces a pruned expert matters; gate renormalization alone costs 0.8–8.5 points on some routers

**What:** Training-free MoE expert pruning decides both which experts to drop and what the layer computes when the router still picks a dropped one. Prior methods change both at once. This paper fixes the pruning mask and compares replacement operators from three families on OLMoE, DeepSeek-V2-Lite and Moonlight, with Granite as a fourth router, using paired, multiplicity-corrected tests. It also proposes the 'tilted novice', a closed-form low-rank map of the pruned expert's input. Its rank-0 case is MoNE's constant replacement.

**Result:** On routers trained without top-k renormalization, renormalizing the surviving gates costs 0.8-8.5 points and flips whether rerouting beats MoNE's mean-output replacement. At 50% pruning, the tilted novice adds 1.2-1.8% parameters and gains 3.45 points over MoNE on OLMoE and 1.37 on Moonlight, robust to 4- and 3-bit quantization. At 25% pruning, MoNE keeps 92-97% of dense multiple-choice accuracy but only 5-65% of GSM8K and 0-17% of code scores. The calibration domain moves the pruning mask 4-9x more than resampling the calibration data does.

**Critique:** This is mainly an evaluation study: no single operator wins everywhere (trained-scale rerouting is best on DeepSeek-V2-Lite), and on two models the tilted novice is statistically indistinguishable from a partial expert copy. The models are small-to-mid MoEs, and nothing tests today's 512-expert-scale models. No code is linked. The finding that multiple-choice accuracy hides large GSM8K and code losses means earlier pruning papers that report only multiple-choice scores may overstate retention. Rests on the Hugging Face paper page.

### 7. [Scheduling Data to Compute: Hierarchical Data Pipelining for LLM Training on AMD GPUs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05751)

arXiv · paper · training

**In one line:** AMD paper combines Tensor Data Mover, transpose-load, SDMA and RDMA to feed LLM training; 1.88x avg kernel speedup from TDM

**What:** The paper argues that the main waste in LLM training is compute units waiting on data held in HBM or on other GPUs. AMD engineers orchestrate the GPU's explicit data-movement hardware into a hierarchical pipeline. The Tensor Data Mover (TDM) streams tensors from HBM to on-chip memory. Transpose-load instructions re-lay data into the layout the math needs as it loads. System DMA and remote DMA move data between GPUs over scale-up and scale-out links, overlapped with compute. They provide kernel- and framework-level implementations in the open-source Primus-Turbo and Primus.

**Result:** Average speedups: TDM 1.88x and transpose-load 1.19x at kernel level; SDMA 1.16x and RDMA 1.33x for compute overlapped with communication. The authors call it the first AMD LLM training system to use all of these data-pipelining features.

**Critique:** The abstract names no baselines, models, model sizes or GPU generation. The speedups are averages over unspecified kernels, and no end-to-end training throughput (tokens/s or MFU) is reported. All 31 authors appear to be from AMD, and nothing compares against equivalent NVIDIA mechanisms (TMA, NVSHMEM). Code is available in the Primus repositories.

### 8. [StateBench: Testing how language models track state changes over long documents](https://huggingface.co/blog/allenai/statebench)

Ai2 blog (Hugging Face) · news · architecture

**In one line:** Ai2 StateBench: 4-level long-context state-tracking benchmark; frontier models ≤13% on the longest hardest problems

**What:** Ai2's benchmark measures how well models track changing entity values over long documents (variable reassignments in code, objects moving in stories). It has four levels: r-trivial (set once), aperiodic (only the latest value matters), soluble (updates build on the current value) and general finite (variables copy each other, as in swaps; tied to the S5 word problem). It evaluates pretrained Olmo 3 (Transformer) and Olmo Hybrid (with Gated DeltaNet layers), frontier models (Gemini 3.8 Flash, Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6 Sol/Luna), and 275M Transformer, hybrid and Gated DeltaNet models trained directly on the task. Published 3 Nov and not covered by earlier runs.

**Result:** Frontier models fall to 13% or lower on the longest general-finite problems. Trained on up to 256 assignments and tested on up to 64,000, Gated DeltaNet and hybrid models generalize almost perfectly on the first three levels. The most expressive Gated DeltaNet variant stays near perfect on the hardest level to about 10x its training length. Transformers degrade with length and never learn the general-finite level.

**Critique:** The architecture comparison uses 275M models trained directly on the task, a long way from pretrained LLMs. The authors themselves say the pretrained models' failures look like a training issue rather than an architecture issue. The benchmark assumes a fixed number of variables, and results depend strongly on problem format (RULER-style vs spreadsheet-style). Data and code are public, so the claims can be checked.

### 9. [You don't need long context](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.05306)

arXiv · paper · inference

**In one line:** NVIDIA paper: context compaction and selective reading match full-history agents within a few points at 2–9x lower estimated cost

**What:** The paper asks whether serving very long contexts is worth the cost, separating long-horizon tasks (history piles up over many actions) from long-input tasks (large source material). It compares three regimes against full history. End-of-Conversation Compaction restarts with a compacted state at a threshold. Single-Action Compaction keeps only a compact semantic state and does one action per context. Selective input reading stores long inputs as files the agent searches with Bash. Tested on 6 long-horizon benchmarks (incl. SWE-bench Verified, Terminal-Bench 2.1, τ²-bench) and 7 long-input ones (incl. LongBench v2, RULER, BABILong). It also proposes, but does not test, 'Semantic State Space Models' that keep an explicit text state.

**Result:** Serving at 256K vs 16K costs 3.6x more per token for GLM 5.2, 5.6x for Kimi K2.6 and 7x for Qwen 3 Coder Next (H100). With compaction on long-horizon tasks, accuracy vs full history ranges -3.7 to +5.6 pp (Claude Opus 4.5) and -3.3 to +4.4 pp (GPT 5.6 Sol), at 2.2-8.8x and 2.4-5.0x estimated savings. Single-Action Compaction on SWE-bench Verified: Opus 70.2 vs 70.0, GPT 82.4 vs 84.4. Peak context falls from 130-200K to 15-37K. Qwen 3 Coder Next collapses from 53.0% to 3.6% on SWE-bench Verified at a 16K/12K budget.

**Critique:** The closed-model cost savings come from a retail-price formula, not measured billing. Results for open-weight models are mixed: several collapse under tight budgets, so the title overstates it for smaller models. Compaction is lossy by construction, and the authors admit their evidence does not rule out tasks that need long context. The NIAH gains come largely from lexical overlap, which the authors themselves call weak evidence. The proposed SSSM idea is untested. URL is the PDF; the abstract page was not retrieved.

### 10. [On the Generalization Limitation of On-Policy Distillation for Post-RL Consolidation](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.05609)

HF Daily Papers · paper · training

**In one line:** Multi-teacher on-policy distillation reaches 96–103% of teachers in-distribution but lands 2.5–16% below base model OOD

**What:** Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) merges several RL-trained specialist teachers into one student and reports near-perfect in-distribution results. This ByteDance-affiliated study tests whether it consolidates the capabilities beyond the supervised distribution. It uses synthetic reasoning tasks and real math, coding, agent and long-context tasks on Qwen3 models, probing extrapolative and compositional generalization. It isolates two bottlenecks, teacher capability and data coverage, analyses the geometry of the updates, and proposes geometry-based patching and base-preserving replay.

**Result:** In extrapolative settings, OPD and MOPD reach 96.04-102.73% of the teachers' in-distribution scores, but their OOD scores are 2.45-16.25% below the base model. On composed tasks, MOPD is 18.19-24.34% below the best teachers. Relieving both bottlenecks together removed the OOD forgetting in their experiments. (M)OPD forgets less OOD than RL baselines on average, with smaller, more spectrally concentrated updates.

**Critique:** The page gives no Qwen3 sizes, no benchmark names and no specific numbers for the proposed fixes. The RL baselines are not named. Removing the forgetting required improving both teacher capability and data coverage, which may not be available in practice. No code is linked yet, only a project page. Rests on the Hugging Face paper page.

### 11. [Muon Makes the First Mixture-of-Experts Layer Harder to Balance](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.37914)

HF Daily Papers · paper · training

**In one line:** Muon roughly doubles the first MoE layer's load-balancing bias span vs AdamW; mean-centering router inputs fixes it

**What:** Open MoE recipes disagree on whether Muon should also train the router. Using the span of each layer's load-balancing biases as an imbalance measure, the paper trains MoE LMs with 55M and 108M active parameters. It compares Muon and AdamW, router-only AdamW, a halved Muon learning rate, and subtracting the batch mean from the first router's inputs during the first 3,000 steps.

**Result:** At learning rates tuned on shorter runs, Muon raised the first MoE layer's bias span at every seed, about 2x on average, with other layers much less affected. The busiest expert received about 4-7x its fair share soon after warmup (the author cites 1.1x under AdamW). The cause is Muon training the rest of the network, whichever optimizer trains the router. Mean-centering the first router's inputs removed the overload with almost no change in loss. Halving Muon's learning rate removed most of it on the smaller model. Moving only the router to AdamW slightly raised the loss.

**Critique:** Tiny scale (55M-108M active) from a single author, with no downstream evaluations, so it is unclear whether this holds for production Muon-trained MoEs. The only baseline is AdamW, and the learning rates were tuned on shorter runs, which may itself cause the effect. On the plus side, code and all 145 runs are public. Published 27 Oct, picked up by HF Daily Papers on 4 Nov, and not covered by earlier runs.
